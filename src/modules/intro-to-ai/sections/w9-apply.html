Application & Access

what AI is used for, and who gets to use it

That first row is Weeks 1–8, exactly. The lecture's move is to point out that every one of those techniques has now been deployed on people, and to ask what follows.

The five key questions — the spine of the whole lecture
  1. How is AI applied in practice? Who has access? Who benefits?
  2. What are the harms and ethical considerations of AI? Who is harmed?
  3. How does bias manifest in AI? Why does it arise?
  4. Who is responsible for the harms of AI?
  5. How do we minimise these harms?

The final slide answers all five in one line each. If you learn one structure from this week, learn this one — it turns any open-ended ethics question into five headings you can write under.

How this week is examined differently
There is nothing to compute here and no worked exercises. Questions are “name and explain”, “classify this case” and “discuss”. What earns marks is using the lecture's own vocabulary — allocative vs representational, direct vs indirect discrimination, the five named causes of bias — rather than general concern. The tools in this week's sections drill exactly those distinctions.

How is AI applied?

Two columns, and two different questions
Well-intentioned applicationsFundamentally problematic applications
Map navigation, music recommendation, phone assistants, computer games, home robots, face detection in photos Military drones, deepfake pornography, criminality prediction from faces, crowd surveillance, phrenology-style facial analysis, oil & gas exploration
“What could be the hidden problems/risks?” “Should we even build this?”

The two questions are not interchangeable, and that is the point of the slide. For the left column, ethics is a matter of mitigation — find the hidden harm and design it out. For the right column, no amount of mitigation is the answer; the question is whether the system should exist. An answer that responds to a right-column case with “use a more balanced dataset” has missed what is being asked.

Applied to policing

Three consequences the lecture names
  • Increased breadth of surveillance.
  • Worse facial recognition performance on minorities due to lack of data → wrongful arrests / stop-and-search.
  • Lack of accountability of AI systems.

Note the causal chain in the middle bullet, because it is the one the exam can ask you to unpack: lack of data → representation bias → worse performance on a group → a real-world allocative harm. Four of this module's concepts in one sentence.

Applied to the military

Two uses, and what follows from each
Search for specific (human) targetsAutomated defense
  • Propagation of bias into war
  • More deaths
  • Dehumanization of war
  • Loss of moral engagement
  • Simplification of complex moral choices
  • Easier escalation
  • Arms races

The automated-defense column is worth reading closely: none of those five harms requires the system to malfunction. They follow from it working exactly as designed. That is a distinction worth making explicitly in an essay answer — most harms in this lecture are failure modes, and these are not.

Applied to political control

Two mechanisms
  • State surveillance of opposition, ethnic and religious minorities → strengthening authoritarian states; human rights losses: privacy, freedom of speech, religious freedom.
  • Increased censorship and disinformation → loss of freedom of speech; less informed citizens; political polarisation and control.
The Week 1 callback
Week 1 introduced the value alignment problem — the difficulty of exactly specifying what “good” means for a system. Weeks 4 and 8 made it numeric: a reward function is the specification. This slide is the same problem at the scale of a state: a system aligned to its operator's values is not thereby aligned to anyone else's. Value alignment appears again as a mitigation method in Part 4, and it is worth being able to connect all three appearances.

The revival of phrenology

The definition to be able to give
Phrenology: a disproved pseudoscience that assumes predictions of gender, intelligence, criminality and internal states can be made from facial analysis.

The lecture's charge is that current AI applications revive it: prediction of gender, sexuality, race, emotion, criminality from images.

Why this is a category error, not just an accuracy problem
These are social constructs, or unobservable — only the subjects can say.

That sentence is the crux, and it is what separates this slide from the rest of the lecture. Everywhere else the complaint is that the system gets the answer wrong for some people. Here there is no ground truth to get right. You cannot fix a sexuality classifier by collecting a more representative dataset, because the label was never a property of the face. This is measurement bias in its purest form — wrong categorization of people — and it is unfixable by construction.

The consequences listed

Such systems allow the oppression of already marginalized communities — the lecture names LGBT and Black communities — through:

  • Reinforcing harmful stereotypes
  • Mis-identification and micro-aggressive ad-targeting
  • The policing case: “our AI system tells us you are feeling guilty, why?!”

That last example is doing real work. It shows the harm arriving even when nobody believes the output is reliable — the mere existence of the claim shifts the burden of proof onto the person. A false accusation you must disprove is a harm regardless of the system's accuracy.

Who has access to AI?

AI is expensive
  • Lots of computation power
  • Expensive hardware (e.g. GPUs)
  • Money to train, money to house
  • Big Tech monopoly (Google, Amazon…)

The result:

  • Universities can't compete, and their resources end up used for Big Tech's priority problems.
  • Poorer labs and countries can't afford it at all.
Why "who has access" is an ethics question and not an economics one
Because access determines which problems get solved. If the only actors who can train large models are a handful of companies, then the research agenda is set by their priorities — and the problems of people those companies do not sell to simply do not get worked on. That is a harm without a villain and without a bug, which is why it needs its own slide.

It also links forward to Part 4: team diversity and participatory design are mitigations aimed at exactly this, an agenda set by too narrow a group.

Exam takeaways

What to have ready
  • The five key questions — application/access, harms, bias, responsibility, minimisation. Use them as headings.
  • Two kinds of application: well-intentioned (“what are the hidden risks?”) and fundamentally problematic (“should we even build this?”). Answer the right question for the case.
  • Policing: breadth of surveillance · worse performance on minorities from lack of data → wrongful arrests · lack of accountability.
  • Military: targeting (bias into war, more deaths); automated defense (dehumanization, loss of moral engagement, simplification of moral choices, easier escalation, arms races).
  • Political control: surveillance of opposition and minorities; censorship and disinformation → polarisation, less informed citizens.
  • Phrenology: define it, then say why prediction of gender/sexuality/race/emotion/criminality from faces revives it — social constructs or unobservable, only the subjects can say.
  • Access: compute, hardware, money, Big Tech monopoly → universities can't compete, poorer labs and countries excluded.
