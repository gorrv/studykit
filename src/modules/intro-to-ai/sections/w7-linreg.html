Supervised learning, and the two tasks inside it

The setting
In supervised learning we capture the patterns of a set of training data with labels, in order to predict new samples.

Take predicting the value of a house. We have data on several houses — size, number of bedrooms, and so on — together with their market value. The challenge is an algorithm that learns how these features should be combined to predict the value of a given, often unseen, house.

Both are supervised: both need labels. The contrast with Week 6 is that clustering had none.

Where the boundary is not where you expect
A decision tree can predict continuous values too — that is Decision Tree Regression, and it is outside the scope of this course. What is in scope is the reverse direction: continuous values can appear as attributes of the data points in a classification problem. Salary, for instance, is a perfectly good feature for predicting credit risk; you split on it with a test like Salary ≤ 30000. That is why every node in the course's figures is labelled with a ≤ test rather than a bare feature name.

Modelling the data: a line plus noise

Equation (7.1)
Before fitting anything, we say where the data came from. Figure 7.1 shows samples drawn from a function but with some error:
y = ½x + ε   (7.1)

Here ε has a normal distribution, ε ∼ N(0, 0.09). The blue points are not on the grey line, and the green dotted segments between point and line are the error, or the residual.

The regression problem, stated exactly
Given the blue points, but not the grey line, how would I estimate the line?

Defining a line is nothing more than defining two parameters:

ŷ = β₀ + β₁x

We write ŷ rather than y to signal that these are predictions, not observed values. β₀ is the intercept; β₁ is the slope.

Three symbols that get mixed up in exam answers
  • y — the value actually observed in the data.
  • ŷ — what the fitted line says y should be at that x.
  • ε — the gap, ε = y − ŷ. Called the error when you are talking about the true generating process, and the residual when you are talking about a fitted line. The notes use both words for the same green segments.

The normal distribution, recapped

The two figures

Left is the data: dots scattered around the line y = ½x. Right is a close-up of the scatter at one x value — at x = 5 the line gives 2.5, and the noise spreads y around it in a bell shape.

µ = 2.5 is where the bell is centred. σ = 0.3 is how wide it is. The shaded band is µ ± σ, which always holds about 68% of the area — whatever µ and σ happen to be.

Figure 7.2, in words
The curve in Figure 7.2 has mean µ = 2.5 and standard deviation σ = 0.3, so by definition the variance is σ² = 0.09. We write normal distributions as N(µ, σ²) — the second argument is the variance, not the standard deviation. Therefore
f(5) ∼ N(2.5, 0.09)

where f(5) denotes the distribution of y when x = 5. That is Figure 7.2 matching the middle bell of Figure 7.1 on purpose: at x = 5 the line y = ½x gives 2.5, and the noise spreads y around it.

Two facts that get asked
  • Normal distributions are continuous, so the chance of getting any one specific value is always zero. You can only talk about intervals, and the probability of landing in one is the area under the curve over that interval.
  • The probability of a value between µ − σ and µ + σ — here 2.2 to 2.8 — is approximately 68%. That number does not depend on µ or σ, which is exactly why σ is worth quoting.

Three points, and we want the line that best approximates them — and then the best value at x = 5. The notes stress the point of the exercise: we want to be able to find it “by hand”.

Step 1 — one residual per point
We minimise the sum of the squares of the residuals. For x = −1, y = 2 we have 2 = β₀ + β₁·(−1) + ε₁, so:
ε₁² = (2 − β₀ + β₁)²
ε₂² = (0 − β₀)²
ε₃² = (0 − β₀ − 2β₁)²

Notice the sign flip on the first line: −β₁xᵢ with xᵢ = −1 becomes +β₁. This is where most by-hand attempts go wrong.

Step 2 — add them up and collect
SSE = (2 − β₀ + β₁)² + (0 − β₀)² + (0 − β₀ − 2β₁)²
      = 4 − 4β₀ + 4β₁ + 3β₀² + 5β₁² + 2β₀β₁

There are two routes from here to the minimum, and which one you take is “down to how much calculus you have seen in school”:

  • Option 1 — partial derivatives. Take ∂SSE/∂β₀ and ∂SSE/∂β₁, set both to zero. (If you go this way you can jump straight to the answers.)
  • Option 2 — parabola vertices. No calculus needed. This is the route the notes take, and it is the one worth being able to reproduce.

Option 2 in full — minimising without calculus

Step 3 — freeze one β and you are left with a parabola
If we make β₁ constant, SSE is a function of β₀ that happens to be a parabola — call it g(β₀). Same in the other direction for h(β₁). Written in the shape f(x) = ax² + bx + c, they are the same six terms sorted differently:
g(β₀) = 3β₀² + (−4 + 2β₁)β₀ + (4 + 4β₁ + 5β₁²)
h(β₁) = 5β₁² + (4 + 2β₀)β₁ + (4 − 4β₀ + 3β₀²)
Step 4 — a parabola is lowest at x = −b/2a
For each β₁ there is a parabola g(β₀) whose minimum we can write down, and vice versa:
β̂₀ = (4 − 2β₁) / 6   (7.2)
β̂₁ = (−4 − 2β₀) / 10   (7.3)

Both β² terms have vanished, so this is now an ordinary pair of simultaneous linear equations.

Step 5 — solve the system
6β₀ = 4 − 2β₁  and  5β₁ = −2 − β₀
6β₀ = 4 − 2(−2 − β₀)/5  ⟹  30β₀ = 24 + 2β₀  ⟹  28β₀ = 24
β̂₀ = 6/7 ≈ 0.86   (7.4)
β̂₁ = −4/7 ≈ −0.57   (7.5)
ŷ = −(4/7)x + 6/7   (7.6)
Two small slips in the printed notes — worth knowing so they do not confuse you
  • Equation (7.6) is introduced as “the best fit line in Figure 7.1”. It is the best fit line of Figure 7.3 — Figure 7.1 is the y = ½x sampling picture, whose line has a positive slope.
  • Equation (7.2) is written with a tilde, β̃₀, in the line above and a hat, β̂₀, in the equation itself. They mean the same thing: the minimising value.

The mathematics is right; only the cross-reference is off. β̂₀ = 6/7 and β̂₁ = −4/7 check out exactly, and the resulting SSE is 8/7 ≈ 1.1429.

The formal definitions

Definition 7.1 — Linear Regression
Let x₁, …, xn ∈ ℝD be a set of points in D dimensions and y₁, …, yn the values associated with them. In a linear regression we look for values β₀, …, βD such that
ŷj = h(xj) = β₀ + β₁xj,1 + β₂xj,2 + ⋯ + βDxj,D   (7.7)

and that best approximate the set of points — in other words, minimise SSE (or MSE) between y₁ … yn and ŷ₁ … ŷn. This is also known as multiple linear regression.

Definition 7.2 — Simple Linear Regression
The same, with D = 1. One feature, two parameters, and everything above applies.
The trap in the word “linear” — this is exam material
“Linear” refers to the parameters βi, not to the variables xi. So polynomial regression,
ŷj = h(xj) = β₀ + β₁xj + β₂xj² + ⋯ + βDxjD

— a curve, obviously — is a special case of linear regression. Treat x, x², x³ as D separate features and Definition 7.1 applies unchanged. This is also the honest answer to “why did the degree-9 curve in Week 5 still count as a linear model”.

Theorem 7.3 — and why it is remarkable

Why squared error at all?
It is not set in stone. Other algorithms minimise the absolute error — look up Least Absolute Deviations. What is nice about SSE is that we can compute a formula that gives us the solution.

This is very rare in machine learning. Not often can we actually prove that a method gives the best solution possible; most algorithms are useful because they work well in practice. K-means, for one, only converges to a local optimum. Linear regression hands you the global one in closed form.

Theorem 7.3 — Solution for Simple Linear Regression
For a simple linear regression yᵢ = β₁xᵢ + β₀ + εᵢ, the least-squares estimates are
β̂₁ = ( n·Σxᵢyᵢ − (Σxᵢ)(Σyᵢ) ) / ( n·Σxᵢ² − (Σxᵢ)² )
β̂₀ = ( (Σxᵢ²)(Σyᵢ) − (Σxᵢ)(Σxᵢyᵢ) ) / ( n·Σxᵢ² − (Σxᵢ)² )

Four sums and a count. Σxᵢ, Σyᵢ, Σxᵢ², Σxᵢyᵢ, and n — the entire dataset reduces to these five numbers as far as the fit is concerned.

The proof, in three lines (explicitly non-examinable)
Differentiate SSE and set both derivatives to zero:
∂SSE/∂β₀ = −2 Σ (yᵢ − β₀ − β₁xᵢ) = 0
∂SSE/∂β₁ = −2 Σ xᵢ(yᵢ − β₀ − β₁xᵢ) = 0

which rearrange into the two normal equations

n·β̂₀ + β̂₁·Σxᵢ = Σyᵢ
β̂₀·Σxᵢ + β̂₁·Σxᵢ² = Σxᵢyᵢ

and solving that 2×2 system gives the theorem. Note what is not used: the normality of the errors. You need that assumption only to prove further properties of the estimators, such as unbiasedness.

A free check on any answer you write
The first normal equation says Σyᵢ = n·β̂₀ + β̂₁Σxᵢ, which is exactly the statement that the residuals sum to zero at the optimum — equivalently, that the fitted line passes through the point of means (x̄, ȳ). Add your residuals up. If they do not cancel, your βs are wrong, and it takes ten seconds to find out.

Exam takeaways

What to have ready
  • Supervised = labelled. Regression predicts a number; classification picks a class. Decision Tree Regression is out of scope, but continuous features in a classification tree are in.
  • N(µ, σ²) carries the variance. σ = 0.3 ⟹ N(µ, 0.09). µ ± σ ≈ 68%. P(exactly one value) = 0 for any continuous distribution.
  • ŷ = β₀ + β₁x; residual εᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ; we minimise Σεᵢ².
  • Know both routes to the minimum. Partial derivatives, or freeze one β and use the vertex of a parabola at −b/2a. The by-hand route is the one the notes work, so it is the one likely to be asked for.
  • Theorem 7.3 is five numbers: n, Σx, Σy, Σx², Σxy. Memorise the shape β̂₁ = (nΣxy − ΣxΣy) / (nΣx² − (Σx)²) and note that β̂₀ reuses the same denominator.
  • “Linear” is about the βs. Polynomial regression is a special case of linear regression.
  • Normality of ε is not used in the proof — only for further properties of the estimators. This is a favourite “state whether the following is true” item.
  • Check your answer by summing the residuals; they must come to zero.
