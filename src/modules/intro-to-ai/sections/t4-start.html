MDPs, Slowly

read this before the other three parts — no equations until the end
If Topic 04 is not going in, it is almost always one of four things
  1. Not seeing what problem any of this solves.
  2. Thinking U(s) means the reward of a square. It does not.
  3. Not noticing that the definition of U is circular, so wondering why we iterate at all.
  4. Meeting the Bellman equation as one long line of symbols instead of four separate ideas.

This page does those four, in that order, with no equation until the last one. Then the formal parts will read like notes rather than code.

1 · The one thing that changed

You press Up. You do not always go up.
In every search problem so far, actions did exactly what they said. Here they do not. You command Up and:
0.8  you go up
0.1  you veer left instead
0.1  you veer right instead

That is the only new ingredient in Topic 04. Everything else — policies, utilities, Bellman, value iteration — is a consequence of it. If you keep that one line in your head, the rest has somewhere to attach.

Why that one change breaks everything you already know
A plan is a list: Up, Up, Right, Right. It only works if step 1 lands you where you expected, so that step 2 still makes sense. With slipping, step 1 might not. Two squares in you are somewhere the list never anticipated, and the rest of it is nonsense.

So you cannot answer “what should I do?” once. You have to answer it for every square you might end up standing on. That answer — one action per state — is a policy, written π(s).

2 · What U(s) actually means

The single most common misreading
U(s) is not the reward of square s. The reward is R(s), and on this grid it is −0.04 almost everywhere — a small fee for taking one more step.

U(s) is the total value of everything that happens from here on, if you stand on s and then behave optimally forever. It bundles up: the fee you pay now, the fees you will pay on the way, and the +1 or −1 you eventually hit.

R(s)U(s)
Iswhat this square pays you right nowwhat standing here is worth in total
Given or computed?given in the problemcomputed — it is the whole answer
On the grid−0.04 nearly everywherenear +1 by the goal, near −1 by the pit

So a square right next to the +1 has a bad reward (−0.04) and a great utility (about 0.9), because of where it lets you go next. Utility is about location, not payout.

3 · Why we cannot just work it out

The circle
Ask what a square is worth and you get:
U(3,3) depends on U(4,3), U(3,2), U(3,3) …
U(3,2) depends on U(3,3), U(3,1), U(4,2) …
U(3,1) depends on U(3,2), U(2,1), U(4,1) …

Every square's value is defined in terms of its neighbours' values, and theirs in terms of it. There is no square you can start from and no substitution that unwinds it. This is not a gap in the notes — the definition really is circular, and that is exactly why an algorithm is needed rather than a formula.

4 · How iteration breaks the circle

Guess, then let the truth spread
  1. Set every non-terminal square to 0. Not because it is worth nothing — because we have to start somewhere, and 0 is as good a lie as any.
  2. The terminals are not a guess. +1 and −1 are given. They are the only true numbers on the board.
  3. Do one sweep: recompute every square from its neighbours' current values.
  4. Repeat until the numbers stop moving.
And here is the bit that makes it click
On the first sweep, almost every square averages over neighbours that are all still 0, so it gets R + γ·0 = −0.04 and learns nothing. The only squares that learn anything are the ones touching a terminal.

On the second sweep, their neighbours can finally see a non-zero number. On the third, the next ring out. One Bellman sweep carries information exactly one square — a square four steps from a terminal cannot possibly move before sweep four. The +1 spreads outward like a stone dropped in water, and value iteration is just waiting for the ripple to reach every corner.

One asymmetry the tool will show you
The +1 spreads visibly. The −1 mostly does not — squares right next to the pit sit on the baseline for several sweeps, looking as though they have heard nothing.

They have heard. It just does not change what they are worth yet. The best action next to a pit is to step away from it, and stepping away costs exactly what any other pointless step costs. Bad news only changes a square's value once it starts closing off the good routes — which happens later, when the surrounding values have risen and avoiding the pit finally means giving something up.

Worth knowing, because it is the sort of thing that looks like a bug in your own hand-computed table when it is not.

5 · One number, computed very slowly

Where 0.76 comes from
Take square (3,3) — one to the left of the +1 goal — on the very first sweep, when every non-terminal square is still 0. Try the action Right.

Step 1 — list what could actually happen. You command Right, so:

with probabilityyou end up atwhose current value iscontribution
0.8(4,3) — the +1 goal10.8 × 1 = 0.80
0.1veer up → off the grid → stay at (3,3)00.1 × 0 = 0
0.1veer down → (3,2)00.1 × 0 = 0
expected value of taking Right0.80

Step 2 — do the same for Up, Down and Left. All three point away from the goal, and every square they can reach is still 0, so they all score 0. Right wins.

Step 3 — add the fee for being here.

U₁(3,3) = R + γ × 0.80 = −0.04 + 1 × 0.80 = 0.76

That is the entire Bellman update, done once, by hand. Value iteration is that calculation, repeated for every square, over and over. Nothing more happens.

Two things that trip people up in that table
  • Bumping into a wall or an edge leaves you where you were. You do not lose a turn or vanish — you stay put, and the value you "land on" is your own current value.
  • The slips are perpendicular to the direction you asked for. Command Right and you can veer up or down — never backwards. That is why every action has exactly three outcomes, not four.

6 · Now the equation, read out loud

Four ideas, not one line
Ui+1(s)  =  R(s)  +  γ  maxa  Σs′ P(s′|s,a) · Ui(s′)
piecesaysin the worked example
R(s)the fee for standing here−0.04
γhow much the future is worth relative to now1 — the future counts fully
maxayou choose, so take the best actionRight beat Up, Down and Left
Σs′ P(·)·U(·)the world chooses, so average over what it might do0.8×1 + 0.1×0 + 0.1×0 = 0.80

Read right to left it is a sentence: average over what the world might do, take the best action you could pick, discount it, and add the fee for being here.

Max over your options, average over the world's. That one line is the Bellman equation, and it is the only thing you have to remember.

Why there is a max at all
Drop the maxa and you have a Markov chain — a world that evolves on its own with nobody steering. The max is the agent. It is the single symbol that turns "watching a process" into "making decisions", and it is what makes this an MDP.

7 · Two things that happen after the numbers settle

Stopping, and then reading off the answer
  • When to stop. Track δ, the largest change any square made this sweep. When δ < ε(1 − γ)/γ, the numbers are close enough and you stop. In practice: stop when nothing is moving.
  • Getting the policy. The utilities are not the answer you wanted — you wanted what do I do? Once U has settled, go to each square and run the same expected-value calculation one last time, but instead of keeping the best number, keep the best action. That is π(s).

So the pipeline is: guess zeros → sweep until still → read off the arrows. Value iteration computes numbers; policy extraction turns them into behaviour.

The one genuinely surprising result in Topic 04
Change R and the optimal policy changes shape, not just slightly. Make the step fee harsh enough and the best policy is to run at the nearest terminal — including the −1 — because dying quickly beats paying the fee forever.

That is Topic 01's value alignment problem arriving as arithmetic. The reward function is the specification: you did not tell it to survive, you told it to maximise a sum, and R = −1.65 quietly means "please die quickly". The reward sweep tool shows exactly where the policy flips.
