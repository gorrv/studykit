Random, Greedy & ε-Greedy

three ways to pick an arm, and only one is a compromise

The 10-armed testbed

The figure

Ten arms. Each shape is one arm's reward distribution — the wider it is at a height, the more likely that reward. The horizontal bar is the arm's true mean q⋆(a). One arm is genuinely best; the agent has to find it by pulling.

What Figure 8.3 shows

Ten arms. Each one pays out with a normal distribution. Two separate random things are going on:

  • How good each arm is. Its true mean was drawn once from N(0, 1).
  • What it pays on a given pull. That varies around its mean, with standard deviation 1.

So one arm really is the best. In the figure it looks like a₅. And it is unique — two arms cannot tie, because drawing the same value twice from a continuous distribution has probability zero.

Two numbers that make Figure 8.4 readable

The arm values came from N(0, 1). So:

  • The average arm is worth 0. A method that hovers at zero has learned nothing.
  • The best arm is worth about 1.54 — that is the expected largest of ten standard normal draws.

Every curve in Figure 8.4 lives between those two numbers. Knowing them turns Exercise 7 from a guess into arithmetic.

The random method

Definition 8.4
Pick an arm uniformly at random, every time:
Pr(At = ai) = 1/k

It does not look at Q at all.

What is wrong with it
It collects information perfectly and then ignores it. Its estimates converge nicely — it just never acts on them.

Its chance of taking the best arm is 1/k on every step, forever. After a million pulls it is still 1/k. For the 50-armed case in Exercise 4 that is 2%.

The greedy method

Definition 8.5
Start with Q₁(a) = 0 for every arm. Then always take the arm with the highest current estimate:
At = arg maxa Qt(a)

If several arms tie for the highest estimate, pick randomly among them.

arg max means "the arm that achieves the maximum" — the arm, not the number.

Three consequences of starting at zero
  • Greedy is not deterministic. At t = 1 every arm sits at 0, so they all tie, and the first pull is a uniform random choice.
  • If rewards are usually positive, greedy gets stuck. The first arm to pay anything above zero beats every untried arm forever. The other k − 1 arms are never pulled once.
  • If rewards are usually negative, the opposite happens. A zero estimate beats any arm you have actually sampled, so greedy keeps switching to untried arms until it has tried them all.

So zero is an optimistic start for negative rewards and a pessimistic one for positive rewards. The notes flag this as a choice, and mention you could instead pull each arm once at the start — “although this is not how we will define it in the context of this lecture”.

The ε-greedy method (epsilon-greedy)

Definition 8.6
Flip a weighted coin before every pull:
  • with probability ε — act at random;
  • with probability 1 − ε — act greedily.

That is the whole method. It is a dial between the two extremes above.

The mistake that costs marks

The random branch picks from all k arms — including the greedy one.

So the chance of taking the greedy arm is not 1 − ε. It is:

(1 − ε) + ε·(1/k)

And the chance of taking any one specific non-greedy arm is ε/k — not zero.

That extra ε/k is exactly what Exercise 5 tests.

The general formula

Let G be the set of arms currently tied for the highest estimate. Then for any arm a:

Pr(At = a) = ε/k  +  (1 − ε)/|G|  if a is in G

Read it as: everyone gets ε/k. The tied leaders split the remaining 1 − ε between them.

So a two-way tie gives each leader (1 − ε)/2, not the full 1 − ε. Ties matter.

Why the smaller ε wins in the long run

The law of large numbers. For any ε > 0, every arm gets sampled infinitely often, so every estimate eventually converges to the truth.

Once that has happened, both methods know which arm is best. The learning is over. What is left is the standing cost of still exploring — and ε = 0.1 randomises ten times as often as ε = 0.01, so it keeps paying more.

Put numerically, once things have settled:

average reward ≈ (1 − ε)·q⋆(best) + ε·(average arm)

On this testbed the average arm is 0, so it is just (1 − ε) × 1.54. Decreasing in ε. That one expression also settles Exercise 7.

Answer the horizon the question asks about
  • “At 500 steps, which is best?” → ε = 0.1.
  • “In the long run, which is best?” → the smallest positive ε.

The crossover happens well past the right edge of the plot. And ε = 0 is excluded from the long-run claim entirely — with no exploration there is no convergence guarantee, because an arm you never pull is an arm you never learn about.

Exam takeaways

What to have ready
  • Random: Pr = 1/k, forever. Greedy: arg max Q, ties randomised, Q₁ = 0.
  • ε-greedy: random with probability ε, greedy otherwise. ε = 0 → greedy, ε = 1 → random.
  • Pr(greedy arm) = 1 − ε + ε/k. Pr(a specific other arm) = ε/k. The ε/k is where the marks are.
  • Ties split the greedy share: |G| leaders each get (1 − ε)/|G| plus ε/k.
  • Figure 8.4: random ≈ 0, greedy stalls, ε = 0.1 leads at 500, ε = 0.01 wins asymptotically — by the law of large numbers.
  • Asymptote ≈ (1 − ε)·q⋆(best), which is (1 − ε) × 1.54 here.
  • ε-greedy's flaw: when it explores it treats every arm equally, including ones it already knows are terrible. That is what UCB fixes.
