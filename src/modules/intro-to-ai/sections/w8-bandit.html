Topic 08 · Part 1 · Reinforcement Learning

Bandits & Action Values

two numbers per machine, and that is the whole algorithm

What reinforcement learning is

In one line
Learn by doing. You try things, you see what you get, and you get better at choosing.

That is the lecture's own phrasing: by repeatedly attempting to solve a problem, the agent learns about it and about the result of each of their actions.

How it differs from Topic 04
In Topic 04 you were told the rewards. You could sit down and compute the answer before moving.

Here nobody tells you anything. The only way to find out what an action is worth is to do it and see. That is the entire difference, and everything in Topic 08 follows from it.

The setup: a row of slot machines

The k-armed bandit

You are in front of k slot machines. Each one pays out a different amount on average, but you do not know which is which. You pull one machine per turn.

Your goal: collect as much as possible in total.

Each pull gives a random amount. Even the best machine sometimes pays badly, and the worst one sometimes pays well. That is what makes this hard.

Example 8.1 — two machines

Two machines, A and B. You press one and get whatever it pays. You can do this as often as you like.

Should you keep pressing the one that has done well so far, or try the other? That question is the whole topic.

The notes suggest A ∼ N(10, 1) and B ∼ N(11, 4) for experimenting. Careful: N(11, 4) means variance 4, so σ = 2. B is better on average but twice as noisy, which is exactly why it takes a while to tell them apart.

The figure

The two arms from Example 8.1. B has the higher mean (11 vs 10), so B is the better arm — but it is twice as spread out, which is exactly what makes it hard to tell them apart from a few pulls. Remember N(11, 4) means variance 4, so σ = 2.

The number you can never see: q⋆(a)

Definition 8.2
q⋆(a) is what machine a really pays on average. Written formally:
q⋆(a) = E[Rt | At = a]

Read it as: the average reward you get, given that you pulled machine a. That is all E[…] means — "the average of".

For A ∼ N(10, 1), q⋆(A) is just 10. The mean of the distribution.

The important part
You never get to see q⋆. It is a fact about the machine, not about you. The agent playing Example 8.1 has no way of learning that A's true mean is exactly 10.

In an exam you are handed q⋆ only so the question can be marked. The star means "the real one" — the thing you are trying to guess.

Why the definition has a t in it
The full version is q⋆(a, t), because in principle a machine could change over time. This course assumes it does not — the machines are stationary — so the t is pointless and we drop it.

The number you actually keep: Qt(a)

Q is a running average. That is genuinely all it is.

For each machine you write down two things:

N(a)  — how many times you have pulled it
Q(a)  — the average of what it has paid you

Those two numbers per machine are the entire state of the algorithm. Nothing else is remembered.

Watch it update — this is Exercise 2

Suppose the pulls go: A pays 0.5, A pays −0.3, B pays 0.4, A pays 1.0, B pays 1.5.

Here is what you know each time you are about to choose:

about to choose…what has happened so farQ(A)Q(B)
step 1nothing00
step 2A paid 0.50.5/1 = 0.50
step 3A paid 0.5, −0.30.2/2 = 0.10
step 4… and B paid 0.40.10.4/1 = 0.4
step 5… and A paid 1.01.2/3 = 0.40.4

Read the Q columns straight down and that is the answer to Exercise 2.

The three things that catch people
  • Qt uses everything before step t, not including it. When you are deciding what to pull at step 5, you have seen steps 1 to 4. So the 1.5 that B pays at step 5 does not appear anywhere in the table above.
  • A machine you have never pulled has Q = 0. Not "unknown", not "blank" — the convention is zero. So Q₁(a) = 0 for everything.
  • Only that machine's own rewards count. Money from B never touches Q(A).
The formula, now that you know what it says
Qt(a) = total paid by a before step t / number of times a was pulled before t

Written with symbols it becomes equation (8.4), and the 1Aᵢ=a in it is just a switch: 1 on the steps where a was chosen, 0 everywhere else. It is there to pick out the right rewards. Nothing more.

The dilemma

The whole problem in two sentences

One machine currently has the best average. Do you keep playing it, or try another one?

Why your averages might be wrong
You may have been unlucky. Pull the best machine twice and get two poor payouts, and its average now looks terrible — so you stop playing it and never find out.

The notes make this point early with A and B: your very first result from a machine could just be an outlier. One sample tells you almost nothing.

Do not look for a threshold
There is no number that says how much exploring is enough. The lecture says so outright: what counts as too much exploration in one situation is too little in another.

What is examinable is the trade-off itself and the named methods that manage it — which is Part 2.

Exam takeaways

What to have ready
  • RL = learn by doing. The rewards are unknown; you find out by acting.
  • k-armed bandit: k machines, one pull per turn, each pays randomly, maximise the total.
  • q⋆(a) = E[Rt | At = a] — the machine's true average. Unknown to the agent. Constant, because we assume stationary.
  • Qt(a) — your running average, from the pulls strictly before step t. Q₁(a) = 0, and 0 for anything never pulled.
  • N(a) — how many times you pulled it. Q and N are the only things you store.
  • 1predicate is a switch: 1 when true, 0 when false. It selects the right rewards in (8.4).
  • N(µ, σ²) carries the variance. N(11, 4) means σ = 2.
  • Exploit = play the current best. Explore = try something else in case you were unlucky. No fixed definition of "too much".
