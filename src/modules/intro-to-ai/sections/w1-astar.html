Topic 01 · Part 3 · Heuristic search

A* & Heuristics

using an estimate of what's ahead

The problem with UCS: it looks backwards

The observation the whole topic turns on
UCS orders the frontier by g(n) — the cost already spent getting to n. That is information about the past. It says nothing about whether n is in the right direction.

So UCS is looking backwards, not forwards: it expands outward from the start in every direction equally, including directly away from the goal.

Heuristic
A rule of thumb — an estimate h(n) of the cost from n to the goal. It's a guess, computed cheaply, that lets the search look forwards.
Where a heuristic actually comes from
On a map, the obvious guess is the straight-line distance to the goal: easy to compute, and always less than or equal to the real road distance. For sliding tiles it might be the number of misplaced tiles. The pattern is the same — solve an easier version of the problem and use its answer as the estimate. That idea gets a name at the bottom of this page.

Greedy best-first search

Definition
f(n) = h(n) — expand whichever frontier node looks closest to the goal. Note h(goal) = 0, so the goal always wins once it's on the frontier.
What it costs you
On the Romania map, greedy best-first returns
ARAD → D → F → BUCHAREST  =  140 + 99 + 211 = 450

The optimal route costs 418. Greedy is fast and gets there, but it is not optimal — it walked into F because F looked close (h = 176), and only then discovered the last leg costs 211.

Why it fails, in one sentence
Greedy throws away g entirely, so it has no memory of what a route has already cost. UCS remembers the past and ignores the future; greedy predicts the future and ignores the past. Both are half-blind — which tells you exactly what to do next.

A*

Definition
f(n) = g(n) + h(n)
  • g(n) — the cost of the path from the start to n (backwards, known).
  • h(n) — the estimated cost from n to the goal (forwards, guessed).
  • f(n) — the estimated cost of the best route through n.
Read f as a question
"If the answer goes through this node, what would the whole trip cost?" Expanding the smallest f means always working on the most promising complete plan, rather than the cheapest fragment (UCS) or the nicest-looking next stop (greedy).

The worked example

Start ARAD, goal BUCHAREST. Straight-line heuristics to BUCHAREST:

ARAD366A329B374 C380D253E193 F176G100H160 I244L241M242 BUCHAREST0

Expanding ARAD gives three children — and f already picks the right one:

A f = 118 + 329 = 447 B f = 75 + 374 = 449 D f = 140 + 253 = 393
Notice what just happened
B is the cheapest child (g = 75) — UCS would expand it first. But B looks far from the goal (h = 374), so its f is the worst of the three. D costs nearly twice as much to reach, yet has the best f. This single step is the difference between UCS and A*.

Continuing — expand D (f = 393), which gives:

C f = 291 + 380 = 671 E f = 220 + 193 = 413 F f = 239 + 176 = 415

E wins (413). Expanding E gives G with f = 317 + 100 = 417 and H with f = 366 + 160 = 526. G (417) still beats F (415)? No — F at 415 is expanded first, offering BUCHAREST at f = 450 + 0 = 450. That's a goal node on the frontier, but A* does not stop: 450 is not the smallest f. G at 417 is. Expanding G offers BUCHAREST at f = 418 + 0 = 418, which is now the smallest — and that is the answer.

ARAD → D → E → G → BUCHAREST  =  140 + 80 + 97 + 101 = 418
The single most common exam mistake
A* does not stop when it generates the goal — only when it expands it. Had it stopped the moment BUCHAREST appeared via F, it would have returned 450, the greedy answer. The optimality guarantee lives entirely in that distinction.

Where do the heuristic numbers actually come from?

The question the table never answers
You are handed h(D) = 253 before you have explored anything. If we do not yet know the route to Bucharest, how can we possibly know it is 253 away?

Because 253 is not the road distance. It is the straight-line distance — measured off the map with a ruler, or computed from the two towns' coordinates. No roads are involved.

Two different pieces of knowledge
g(n)h(n)
What it isthe cost of the road you have actually travelledthe straight-line distance still to go
How you get itby adding up edges as you searchfrom the coordinates, before you search at all
Exact or estimate?exact — you drove itan estimate, and always an under-estimate
Changes during search?yes, it grows as you gono — it is a fixed property of the map

You know where Bucharest is. You do not know how to drive there. Those are separate facts, and A* uses the cheap one to find the expensive one.

The intuition, if the map is not helping
You are lost in a city and you can see a cathedral spire about two kilometres away. You know it is two kilometres away. You have no idea how to walk there — one-way streets, a river, dead ends. The two kilometres is h. The distance you actually end up walking is g, and it will be more than two kilometres.
Why the ruler is always wrong in the same direction
A straight line is the shortest thing between two points, so no road can beat it. Check it on D:
h(D) = 253   the ruler
true road = D → E → G → BUCHAREST = 80 + 97 + 101 = 278
253 ≤ 278   the estimate is 25 too low, as it must be

Never too high. That one-sided error is exactly the admissibility condition, and it is the whole reason A* can be trusted to return the cheapest path.

Two things worth knowing for the exam
  • You never compute h yourself. It is given to you in a table, exactly as in the worked example above. Computing it is the map-maker's job, not the search algorithm's.
  • When there are no coordinates to measure — an 8-puzzle, a planning problem — you get h by relaxing the problem: solve an easier version and use its cost as the estimate. Counting misplaced tiles is the relaxation "any tile may teleport". Topic 03's RPG is the relaxation "actions have no delete effects". Same trick, twice.

Three things to actually try in it

  2. Switch to f(n) = h(n). Greedy dives at whatever looks closest and stops caring what it has already spent — compare the final cost with A*'s.
  3. Drag h(D) up past its true remaining distance. D stops looking attractive, A* explores elsewhere, and the path it returns may no longer be the cheapest. That is what losing admissibility costs you.

Admissible heuristics

Definition
A heuristic is admissible if it never over-estimates the cost to the goal — that is, for every node,
actual cost to goal  ≥  h(n)
  • Straight-line distance is admissible — no road is shorter than the straight line.
  • A* with an admissible heuristic is optimal.
  • h ≡ 0 is admissible — but then f = g and A* degenerates into UCS.
Why over-estimating breaks it
If h over-estimates at some node, f at that node is too high, so A* may leave it sitting on the frontier and expand a goal through a worse route instead. In the worked example, if h(G) were 150 instead of 100, then f(G) = 467 > 450 and A* would have returned the 450 route. Over-estimating is exactly the failure that admissibility rules out.
The key challenge
Find a heuristic that is admissible AND informative.
  • Admissible alone is easy — h ≡ 0 qualifies, and is useless.
  • Informative means close to the true cost, so the search is strongly guided.
  • The higher h is without ever exceeding the truth, the fewer nodes A* expands.

How to get one: by relaxing the constraints of the problem — solve an easier version and use its cost as the estimate.

Relaxation, made concrete
Straight-line distance is a relaxation: drop the constraint "you must follow roads" and you get flying, whose cost is the straight line. For sliding tiles, drop "a tile can only move into the gap" and you get "number of misplaced tiles"; drop only "the target square must be empty" and you get the sum of Manhattan distances — a stronger, still-admissible heuristic. Removing a constraint can only make things cheaper, which is precisely why a relaxed solution never over-estimates.

Exam-style takeaways

Key things to nail for Part 3
  1. UCS looks backwards — g is cost already spent, so UCS has no sense of direction.
  2. Heuristic = a rule of thumb estimating the cost from a node to the goal.
  3. Greedy best-first — f(n) = h(n), h(goal) = 0; on the map it returns 140 + 99 + 211 = 450, which is not optimal.
  4. A* — f(n) = g(n) + h(n): cost so far plus estimated cost remaining = estimated cost of the best route through that node.
  5. Be able to compute f-values by hand — from ARAD: A 447 = 118 + 329, B 449 = 75 + 374, D 393 = 140 + 253; then C 671, E 413, F 415; then G 417, H 526; goal via F = 450, via G = 418.
  6. The optimal path — ARAD → D → E → G → BUCHAREST = 140 + 80 + 97 + 101 = 418.
  7. A* stops when it expands the goal, not when it generates it — otherwise it would return 450.
  8. Admissible = never over-estimates; actual cost ≥ h(n); straight-line distance is admissible; A* + admissible ⇒ optimal.
  9. h ≡ 0 is admissible but useless — A* becomes UCS.
  10. The key challenge — admissible and informative; obtained by relaxing the constraints of the problem.
