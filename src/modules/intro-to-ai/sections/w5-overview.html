Machine Learning: Overview

the other half of the field, finally

What machine learning is

The classic definition
"The field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without being explicitly programmed."

— Arthur Samuel, 1959

This is the right-hand column of Topic 01, at last
Topic 01 split AI into model-based (explicit knowledge, symbolic, classical — "pretends to think") and data-driven (tacit knowledge, machine learning — "pretends to learn"). Topics 1–4 were entirely the left-hand column: search, adversarial search, CSPs, planning, MDPs — all of them require you to write the world down first.

Samuel's phrase "without being explicitly programmed" is precisely the boundary. In a PDDL domain you state that loading a cargo deletes at(c,l). In machine learning nobody states anything; the rule is extracted from examples.

The running example
A classification problem: muffin or chihuahua? A grid of photos where blueberry muffins and chihuahua faces are genuinely, uncomfortably similar.

Note what makes it a good example: nobody can write down the rule. You cannot state, in code, what distinguishes a muffin from a small dog — but you can produce ten thousand labelled photos. That gap is the entire reason machine learning exists.

They are not four different things — they are four angles on one thing
  • Samuel — what it is not: hand-written rules.
  • Mohri et al. — the mechanism: experience (data) improving performance. Note it takes a measurable view — you must be able to say how good you are, which is Part 2.
  • Russell & Norvig — the dual nature of the product: a model is both a hypothesis (a claim about how the world works) and a program (something you can run). That's a very AI-textbook framing.
  • Murphy — the statistician's view: detect patterns, then predict future data.

If an exam asks "define machine learning", give one verbatim and attribute it, then say what it emphasises. Naming the source is free marks.

"For the mathematicians out there…"

The honest caveat
For several machine learning algorithms, there are no mathematical proofs that they actually work.

The point is often illustrated with the xkcd cartoon (#1838) in which a machine learning system is a pile of linear algebra, and the fix for wrong answers is to stir the pile until they start looking right.

Why this matters more than it sounds
Everything in Topics 1–4 came with guarantees: A* is optimal if h is admissible; alpha-beta returns exactly the minimax value; AC-3 terminates; value iteration converges to the unique fixpoint. You could prove all of them.

Machine learning largely cannot offer that. So the field replaces proof with measurement — you cannot show the model is right, so you must show it performs well on data it has never seen. That substitution is what Parts 2 and 3 are about, and it's why they take up two thirds of the topic.

Three types of machine learning

Learn all three definitions
  • Supervised learning — the program is 'trained' on a given set of examples (with labels). It learns how to reach an accurate conclusion when given new data.
  • Unsupervised learning — the program is given a bunch of unlabelled data and must discover patterns and relationships in them.
  • Reinforcement learning — the program learns from the consequences of its actions (reward or punishment), rather than from being explicitly taught, and selects its actions on the basis of its past experiences (exploitation) and also by new choices (exploration).
The distinguishing question is "where does the feedback come from?"
  • Supervised — someone hands you the right answer for every example.
  • Unsupervised — there is no right answer; you're looking for structure.
  • Reinforcement — no one tells you the right answer, but the world eventually tells you whether things went well.
You have already met reinforcement learning's world — from the other side
Reward, punishment, actions, past experience, exploration versus exploitation: this is Topic 04's MDP vocabulary exactly. The difference is what you're given.
  • In an MDP you are handed P(s′|s,a) and R(s) and you compute the optimal policy with value iteration.
  • In reinforcement learning you are handed neither. You must learn the policy by acting and observing what happens.

And that's why exploration vs exploitation exists in RL but never appeared in Topic 04: when you already know the transition model, there is nothing to explore.

Four classes of learning problem

ProblemTypeWhat it does
ClassificationsupervisedOrganises data in classes and determines the class of new data-points.
RegressionsupervisedFits functions to data and determines values of new data-points.
ClusteringunsupervisedSeparates data in groups and determines the group of new data-points.
Dimensionality reductionunsupervisedTransforms high-dimensional data into lower-dimensional data while preserving desired properties.
Two pairs, two distinctions
Classification vs regression — both supervised; the difference is the output type. Classification predicts a label from a fixed set (muffin / chihuahua); regression predicts a number (house price, temperature). That difference is exactly why they need different metrics in Part 2 — you cannot take the "mean absolute error" of "chihuahua".

Classification vs clustering — these look identical and are not. Classification is given the classes and told which points belong to which. Clustering is given neither and has to invent the groups. "Determines the class of new data-points" versus "determines the group" is the giveaway wording.

Exam-style takeaways

Key things to nail for Part 1
  1. Samuel 1959 — "the field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without being explicitly programmed". Know the quote and the year.
  2. The other three definitions and their sources — Mohri et al. 2018 (experience → performance), Russell & Norvig 2022 (observes data, builds a model that is both hypothesis and software), Murphy 2012 (detect patterns, predict future data).
  3. Supervised = trained on labelled examples. Unsupervised = unlabelled data, discover patterns. Reinforcement = learns from reward or punishment, balancing exploitation of past experience against exploration of new choices.
  4. Classification and regression are supervised; clustering and dimensionality reduction are unsupervised. Be able to state what each does.
  5. Classification vs regression — label versus number. Classification vs clustering — the classes are given versus the groups are discovered.
  6. Many ML algorithms have no mathematical proof that they work — which is why empirical evaluation on held-out data is so central.
  7. Where this sits — this is the data-driven / tacit knowledge half of Topic 01's split, after four topics entirely in the model-based half.
