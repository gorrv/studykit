Exercise 1 · within-cluster variance

Recall the cluster options from Example 6.1. Either C̃₁ = {p₁,p₂,p₃,p₄} and C̃₂ = {p₅,p₆}, or Ĉ₁ = {p₁,p₂,p₃} and Ĉ₂ = {p₄,p₅,p₆}. Calculate V(C̃₁), V(C̃₂) and V(Ĉ₁). Then use V(Ĉ₂) too, to determine which of the two cluster assignments has the lower within-cluster variance.

V(C̃₁), where C̃₁ = {p₁,p₂,p₃,p₄} — four points, so 6 pairs, divided by 4:

d(p₁,p₂)² = (1−2)² + (2−3)² = 1 + 1 =  2
d(p₁,p₃)² = (1−3)² + (2−0)² = 4 + 4 =  8
d(p₁,p₄)² = (1−4)² + (2−4)² = 9 + 4 = 13
d(p₂,p₃)² = (2−3)² + (3−0)² = 1 + 9 = 10
d(p₂,p₄)² = (2−4)² + (3−4)² = 4 + 1 =  5
d(p₃,p₄)² = (3−4)² + (0−4)² = 1 + 16 = 17
sum = 55  →  V(C̃₁) = 55 / 4 = 13.75

V(C̃₂), where C̃₂ = {p₅,p₆} — one pair, divided by 2:

d(p₅,p₆)² = (6−7)² + (7−6)² = 1 + 1 = 2
V(C̃₂) = 2 / 2 = 1.00

V(Ĉ₁), where Ĉ₁ = {p₁,p₂,p₃} — three pairs, divided by 3:

2 + 8 + 10 = 20  →  V(Ĉ₁) = 20 / 3 ≈ 6.67

And V(Ĉ₂) ≈ 9.33 is given in the notes.

Answer
AssignmentV of cluster 1V of cluster 2Total
C̃ — {p₁,p₂,p₃,p₄} / {p₅,p₆}13.751.0014.75
Ĉ — {p₁,p₂,p₃} / {p₄,p₅,p₆}6.679.3316.00
C̃ has the lower total within-cluster variance (14.75 < 16.00), so p₄ should join {p₁,p₂,p₃}.
Why the answer is counter-intuitive, and worth a sentence in an exam
Look at the picture: p₄ = (4,4) is closer to the centre of {p₅,p₆} than to the centre of {p₁,p₂,p₃}. Ĉ₁ on its own is also far tighter than C̃₁ (6.67 vs 13.75). Yet Ĉ loses.

The reason is the 1/|Ck| normalisation. Adding p₄ to the trio adds three new pair-terms (13 + 5 + 17 = 35) but also raises the divisor from 3 to 4. Adding it to the pair adds two terms (13 + 13 = 26) and raises the divisor only from 2 to 3, which is proportionally much worse. Dividing by the number of points, not the number of pairs, systematically favours putting the awkward point in the bigger cluster.

Exercise 3 · the same run with the indices swapped

Same data points, K = 2, but now invert the cluster indexes: µ₁(0) = (6,7) and µ₂(0) = (1,2). What are C₁(1), C₂(1), µ₁(1), µ₂(1)? What about iteration 2?

Every distance is identical to Exercise 2 — the two centres are the same two points, just labelled the other way round. So p₁, p₂, p₃ go to C₂ and p₅, p₆ go to C₁.

And p₄ is still exactly tied — 13 from both
But now the lowest index is C₁, which is the (6,7) centre. The same tie-breaking rule that sent p₄ down-left in Exercise 2 sends it up-right here. This single line is the entire exercise.
Answer — iteration 1
C₁(1) = {p₄, p₅, p₆}    C₂(1) = {p₁, p₂, p₃}
µ₁(1) = ((4+6+7)/3, (4+7+6)/3) = (17/3, 17/3) ≈ (5.67, 5.67)
µ₂(1) = ((1+2+3)/3, (2+3+0)/3) = (2, 5/3) ≈ (2.00, 1.67)

Iteration 2 — check p₄ = (4,4) again: squared distance to µ₁ is 2.78 + 2.78 = 5.56, to µ₂ is 4 + 5.44 = 9.44. It stays in C₁. Nothing moves.

Answer — iteration 2
C₁(2) = {p₄, p₅, p₆}    C₂(2) = {p₁, p₂, p₃}
µ₁(2) ≈ (5.67, 5.67)    µ₂(2) ≈ (2.00, 1.67)  — unchanged, so it terminates.
The point of the exercise
Same data, same K, same algorithm, the only difference is which centre got called number 1 — and K-means converges to a different answer, with total within-cluster variance 16.00 instead of 14.75.

Both runs are stable: neither can improve by moving a single point, so both are genuine local minima. Exercise 1 already told us 14.75 is the better of the two, so Exercise 3's run has landed on a local minimum that is not global — which is exactly what Section 6.2.4 then goes on to discuss, and why n_init and k-means++ exist.

Exercise 4 · Manhattan distance

Compute the Manhattan distance between p₁ = (3, 6, 1, −1) and p₂ = (−3, 6, 2, 5).

‖p₁ − p₂‖₁ = |3 − (−3)| + |6 − 6| + |1 − 2| + |−1 − 5|
            =    6   +   0   +  1   +   6
Answer13
Two easy marks to lose
The second coordinate contributes 0 — it is the same in both points, and it is easy to skip a term when one vanishes. And |−1 − 5| = |−6| = 6, not 4: subtract first, then take the modulus.

For comparison, the Euclidean distance here is √(36 + 0 + 1 + 36) = √73 ≈ 8.54 — always ≤ the Manhattan distance, which is a useful sanity check.

Exercise 5 · how long does it run?

If n > 1 is the number of initial points in a hierarchical clustering algorithm, what is the maximum time t until the algorithm ends?

We start at t = 0 with n clusters and stop when there is 1. Step 2 always merges at least two clusters, so every step reduces the cluster count by at least one. To get from n down to 1 therefore takes at most n − 1 steps.

Answert = n − 1
Why the question says maximum
Because of the tie rule in Remark 6.11. If several pairs sit at the threshold, one step can remove two or more clusters at once, and the run finishes early. So n − 1 is the upper bound, achieved exactly when every step merges a single pair — which is what happens in the chapter's example: n = 6 and it takes 5 steps.

This is also the reason a dendogram of n points has at most n − 1 join heights, and why some values of K may be skipped.

Exercise 6 · single vs complete linkage

The example in this section used single-linkage and L₁. The clustering process differs if we use complete-linkage instead (keeping Manhattan distance). What is the highest value of K for which the algorithms differ? For K = 6 the clustering is the same; for K = 5, although the distances change, the actual cluster assignment is the same. What is the highest K for which it isn't?

Run both from the same distance table. The first two merges agree, because at that stage the clusters involved are singletons or their nearest pair is unambiguous:

AnswerK = 3

Where they part company. At K = 4 the clusters are {1,5}, {2,3}, {4}, {6}. Now compare the two candidate merges:

single:   dist({2,3},{6}) = min(7, 12) = 7  ·  dist({4},{6}) = 8  → merge {2,3} with {6}
complete: dist({2,3},{6}) = max(7, 12) = 12  ·  dist({4},{6}) = 8  → merge {4} with {6}
The one number that does it
d(p₃, p₆) = 12. Single-linkage ignores it — {2,3} is only 7 from {6} because p₂ is close — so {6} joins the pair. Complete-linkage is forced to use it, which pushes {2,3} out to 12 and lets {4} win at 8 instead.

Note the two then re-converge at K = 2. Different intermediate structures can lead to the same final split, so "the algorithms differ" has to be checked at every K, not just the end.

Exercise 7 · the helicopter problem

Cities are points, with Euclidean distances. Your helicopters have limited fuel and can travel non-stop between cities at most 100 miles apart. You want to cluster cities so that a helicopter can go from any city to any other city within the same cluster without stopping for fuel. You set the threshold to 100. Which criterion should you use?

AnswerComplete-linkage.

Why. The requirement is about every pair of cities in a cluster, not some pair. Complete-linkage defines the distance between two clusters as the maximum over cross-cluster pairs, so it only merges when the furthest apart pair is within the threshold. Merging at threshold 100 therefore guarantees every pair inside a cluster is at most 100 miles apart — precisely the guarantee the helicopters need.

Why single-linkage would strand a helicopter
Single-linkage merges when the closest pair is within 100. So take three cities in a line: A is 90 from B, B is 90 from C, and A is 180 from C. Both of those 90s are the joint-smallest distance, so under the tie rule single-linkage merges all three in one step at threshold 90 — comfortably under your limit of 100. A helicopter then asked to fly A → C runs out of fuel halfway. That is chaining, and here it is not a stylistic quirk but a crash.

Average-linkage fails for the same reason, more subtly: an average below 100 says nothing about the worst pair.

The general principle worth stating in an answer
Match the linkage to the guarantee you need. Complete-linkage gives you a bound on the diameter of every cluster; single-linkage gives you a bound on connectivity — that you can get between any two members via a chain of short hops. If refuelling were allowed at intermediate cities, single-linkage would be the right answer.
