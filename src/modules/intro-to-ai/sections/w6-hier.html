Hierarchical Clustering

every K at once, from n clusters down to one

The idea

The motivation
Instead of finding a fixed number of clusters, we create a process in which the number of clusters progressively changes as steps go by. We can then stop when we reach the desired number of clusters.

The process is simple: at step 1 there are K = n clusters — each point is its own cluster. At each time-step, we select the closest pair of clusters and merge them into one.

Informally, the algorithm
  1. At the start, assign each point to their own cluster.
  2. Merge the two closest clusters according to a given criterion.
  3. If all points are in one cluster, stop. Otherwise, go back to step 2.
Two things to pin down before you can run it
  • Linkage criterion — how do we define distance between two clusters?
  • Metric — which metric do we use to define distance between two points?

These are related, since both reason about distances. But — and this is the point to remember — the criterion for cluster distance is independent of the choice of metric. Any of the three linkages combines with any metric, giving the six dendograms at the end of this page.

The trade against K-means, in one line each
K-means: you must choose K up front, it can land in a bad local minimum, and the answer depends on the initialisation. Hierarchical: no K needed, no initialisation, and it is deterministic — but it is greedy, so an early merge can never be undone, and it is far more expensive (you recompute a whole distance table every step).

The two metrics

Definition 6.5 — Manhattan, L₁

‖p₁ − p₂‖₁ := Σi=1D |p1i − p2i|

The sum of the absolute coordinate differences, where |x| is the usual modulus. Also called the L₁ norm — hence the subscript 1.

Definition 6.6 — Euclidean, L₂

‖p₁ − p₂‖₂ := ( Σi=1D (p1i − p2i)² )½

Straight-line distance. Also called the L₂ norm.

Note the square root is back
Part 1's V(Ck) used squared Euclidean distance with no root. These definitions are the genuine norms, root and all. Hierarchical clustering compares distances rather than summing them into an objective, so there is no reason to drop the root — and dropping it would change nothing about the order of merges anyway.

The three linkage criteria

Definitions 6.7, 6.8 and 6.9
Let C₁ and C₂ be two clusters and ‖·‖ a norm (L₁ or L₂, say).
CriterionDefinitionIn words
Single-linkage distSL(C₁,C₂) := minp∈C₁, q∈C₂ ‖p − q‖ the shortest distance between points from one cluster to the other
Complete-linkage distCL(C₁,C₂) := maxp∈C₁, q∈C₂ ‖p − q‖ the longest distance between points from one cluster to the other
Average-linkage distAL(C₁,C₂) := (1/mn) Σi=1n Σj=1m ‖pi − qj‖ the average distance between points from one cluster to the other

Note that mn is the number of pairs of points between clusters — every point of C₁ against every point of C₂.

What each one actually does to the shape of your clusters
  • Single-linkage only needs one close pair, so clusters can grow along a chain of near-neighbours — long, straggly shapes. Good at finding elongated structure, notoriously prone to "chaining" two blobs together through a bridge of stray points.
  • Complete-linkage requires every pair to be close before it merges, so it produces tight, compact, roughly equal-sized clusters — and refuses to merge anything with an outlier hanging off it.
  • Average-linkage sits between the two and is the usual compromise.

This is not cosmetic. On the notes' own six points, single and complete linkage produce genuinely different clusterings at K = 3, which is Exercise 6. And the difference is exactly what Exercise 7's helicopter question turns on.

Where single-linkage first bites
At iteration 1 we need the distance from {1,5} to {4}. Should that be d(p₁,p₄) = 16 or d(p₅,p₄) = 12? "The former is worse, the latter is better, so what is the correct one? Well, that is where the criterion comes in."

Single-linkage takes the shortest distance between any two points, one in each cluster — so 12.

The formal algorithm, and what happens on ties

Definition 6.10 — Hierarchical Clustering Algorithm
  1. Create n clusters, one per data point. At t = 0, C₁ = {p₁} … Cn = {pn}. Move time to t = 1.
  2. At time t, merge clusters that have the smallest distance between each other, according to the given criterion. Evaluate, over every distinct pair:
    threshold(t) := minCi,Cj dist(Ci(t), Cj(t))   (6.11)
    If only one pair achieves it, merge them (assuming i < j): Ci(t+1) = Ci(t) ∪ Cj(t). In case of ties, merge all clusters that are a distance of threshold(t) away from each other.
  3. Stop if there is only one cluster containing all points. Otherwise go back to step 2 with t + 1.

Naming convention: the lower index at time t is used when naming the merged cluster at t + 1.

Remark 6.11 — the two tie scenarios
  1. Two separate pairs. C₁ and C₂ are 10 apart, and C₃ and C₄ are also 10 apart, and 10 is the smallest distance overall. Then perform two merges: C₁∪C₂ and C₃∪C₄. K drops by 2 in one step.
  2. A chain. C₁ and C₂ are 10 apart and C₂ and C₃ are also 10 apart. Then merge all three into one big cluster. K drops by 2 here too, but into a single cluster rather than two.
The honest caveat, and the consequence
The notes are explicit that "there are no correct answers for the points we raised… there are often arguments both ways". Merging every pair at the threshold has a real cost:

it makes it impossible to have all values of K — you can "jump" from x clusters to x − 2 or fewer, so some K simply never occurs. On the other hand, it ties really nicely into dendograms, because everything at the same height merges at the same height.

If an exam asks you to justify the tie rule, that is the trade: lose some values of K, gain a clean dendogram.

Dendograms

The figure

Complete linkage, L₂, on the six points from Part 1. The height of each bar is the distance at which that merge happened. Read it bottom-up: p1+p2 and p5+p6 join cheaply at 1.41, then p3 joins at 3.16, then p4 joins p5p6 at 3.61 — and the last merge costs 7.62. That big jump is where to cut, and cutting there gives K = 2.

What they are for
A dendogram visualises the entire hierarchical clustering process at once. Leaves are points; each horizontal join is a merge; the height of a join is the threshold at which it happened.

They also help decide a suitable K. If we need to increase the threshold a lot before the next pair merges, that indicates the clusters were 'far away'. Long vertical parts of the dendogram mark those points.

The notes' worked reading — Figure 6.9, complete linkage with L₁
"It looks like K = 2 may be a good choice, since one needs to increase the threshold from around 20 to around 35 to go from 2 clusters to 1 cluster."

Running that combination gives merge thresholds 4, 5, 8, 20, 36. The jump from 20 to 36 is the long vertical bar at the top — by far the biggest gap in the sequence — so cutting just below it, at K = 2, is the natural choice.

Exam-style takeaways

Key things to nail for Part 2
  1. Start with n singleton clusters; repeatedly merge the closest pair; stop at one cluster.
  2. Two independent choices — the linkage criterion (cluster-to-cluster) and the metric (point-to-point). Any combination is legal.
  3. L₁ (Manhattan) = Σ|p₁ᵢ − p₂ᵢ|. L₂ (Euclidean) = (Σ(p₁ᵢ − p₂ᵢ)²)½. Write both.
  4. Single = min, complete = max, average = mean over all cross-cluster pairs; average divides by mn, the number of such pairs.
  5. Be able to build the distance table and run the merges by hand. On the notes' points with single + L₁: thresholds 4, 5, 7, 8, 12, merging {1,5}, then {2,3}, then {2,3,6}, then {2,3,4,6}, then everything.
  6. Ties (Remark 6.11) — merge all clusters at the threshold; two separate pairs give two merges, a chain gives one big cluster. The cost is that some values of K become unreachable.
  7. Dendograms — leaves are points, join height is the merge threshold; a long vertical gap indicates a good place to cut, i.e. a good K.
  8. The maximum number of steps is n − 1 (Exercise 5) — each merge reduces the cluster count by at least one.
  9. Single-linkage chains; complete-linkage keeps clusters compact. This is the reasoning Exercise 7 wants.
