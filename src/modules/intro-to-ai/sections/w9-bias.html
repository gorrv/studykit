The three definitions

Direct and indirect discrimination — learn both names for each
Direct discrimination
“disparate treatment”		Indirect discrimination

Definition when somebody is disadvantaged because of a personal attribute (e.g. age, gender, race) when people with a certain personal attribute are disadvantaged even though the attribute is not explicitly considered in decisions
Is the attribute an input?YesNo — and this is the point
Examplea model that takes race as a feature and scores accordinglya model that takes postcode, which correlates with race

The exam-relevant consequence: deleting the protected attribute from your feature set does not make a system fair. It rules out direct discrimination and leaves indirect discrimination completely untouched — and indirect is the kind almost every real AI case turns out to be.

Bias, in the context of AI ethics
The lecture gives it two senses, and you should be able to say which you mean:
  • Imbalance or tendency in data — the input sense.
  • Direct or indirect discrimination — the output sense.

These are connected but not the same, and conflating them is a common source of muddle: imbalanced data does not automatically produce discrimination, and discrimination can arise from perfectly balanced data (via the label, the objective, or the metric). The five causes on the next slide are precisely the routes from one to the other.

Four documented examples

1 · Recidivism prediction — ProPublica, “Machine Bias”
The algorithm was more likely to mistakenly predict high-risk on Black than on White defendants.

Read the sentence carefully, because the precision matters: the claim is about mistaken high-risk predictions — a difference in the false positive rate between groups, not simply a difference in scores. Two groups can receive different score distributions for entirely legitimate reasons; being wrongly flagged at different rates is harder to defend.

In the taxonomy: an allocative harm (distribution of opportunities — liberty, bail), arising from measurement bias if the label is re-arrest, and it is indirect discrimination since race is not an input.

2 · Generative models — GPT-3 and DALL·E
The slide shows outputs from both, with the caption “All white male.”

This is a representational harm rather than an allocative one — nobody is denied a resource. Stereotyping and erasing are the relevant sub-types. It is worth noticing that this failure has no victim who can point to a decision made about them, which is exactly why the reparation list in Part 2 works so poorly for it.

3 · Pedestrian detection — Brandao (2019)
“Age and gender bias in pedestrian detection algorithms.” The slide shows miss rates differing by age and gender group.

A quality-of-service allocative harm with an obvious physical safety consequence — a detector that misses children more often is a safety harm, not merely an accuracy footnote. This is the clearest case of one harm category producing another.

4 · Gender classification — Buolamwini & Gebru, “Gender Shades” (2018)

The slide reproduces the error-rate table across Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI for three commercial systems:

systemType IType IIType IIIType IVType VType VI
Microsoft1.7%1.1%3.3%0%23.2%25.0%
Face++11.9%9.7%8.2%13.9%32.4%46.5%
IBM5.1%7.4%8.2%8.3%33.3%46.8%

What to take from the numbers. Two of the three systems are close to a coin flip on the darkest skin type — 46.5% and 46.8% error on a binary classification. And the best system goes from 0% error on Type IV to 25% on Type VI. No single overall accuracy figure would have shown any of this, which is the whole argument for disaggregated evaluation.

Why "intersectional" is in the paper's title
The disparity is far larger for combinations of attributes than for either attribute alone. A system can look nearly even across gender overall and nearly even across skin type overall, and still fail catastrophically for the group at the intersection of both. Checking one attribute at a time is not enough — and this is a good, specific point to make in an answer about evaluation.

The five causes of discrimination by AI

Branch 1 — the risk was not anticipated, tested, or alleviated
causethe lecture's descriptiondeveloper's fault?
World biasworld distribution problemno
Representation biasdata collection problem★ yes
Measurement biaswrong categorization of people, or wrong measurements★ yes
Algorithm biaswrong choice of algorithm★ yes
Evaluation biaswrong choice of evaluation metric or test set★ yes

Four of the five carry the developer's-fault marker. Only world bias does not — because the data is an accurate record of an unequal world, so no amount of better collection fixes it. That single exception is an easy thing to be asked about, and the answer is not “so it's nobody's fault”: deploying a system that reproduces world bias is still a decision somebody made.

Branch 2 — the risk is obvious, the task is problematic
failuredeveloper's fault?
Ethics board does not flag a problem—
Developer doesn't oppose building it, doesn't report, doesn't blow the whistle★ yes
Management makes the decision to deploy—

This is the branch for the right-hand column of Part 1 — cases where the question was “should we even build this?” and the answer was no. The developer's fault here is not a technical failure but a failure to object, which is why whistleblowing appears in the mitigation list in Part 4.

Why this slide sits here rather than in the mitigation section
Because it is an empirical claim about how these findings actually got made, not a recommendation. Gender Shades was authored by Buolamwini and Gebru; the disparities had been sitting in deployed commercial products, measurable by anyone, for years before someone thought to break the numbers down that way.

The argument it supports is a practical one: you cannot test for a harm you have not imagined, and what you imagine is bounded by who is in the room. That is why team diversity and participatory design appear as mitigations in Part 4 — they are not gestures at fairness, they are proposed as error-detection mechanisms. Making that argument explicitly is worth more than asserting that diversity is good.

Exam takeaways

What to have ready
  • Direct discrimination = disparate treatment — disadvantaged because of a personal attribute. Indirect = disparate impact — disadvantaged even though the attribute is not explicitly considered.
  • Removing the protected attribute does not prevent indirect discrimination, and most real cases are indirect.
  • Bias has an input sense (imbalance or tendency in data) and an output sense (direct or indirect discrimination).
  • The five causes: world · representation · measurement · algorithm · evaluation. All but world bias are marked the developer's fault.
  • Second branch: obvious risk, problematic task — ethics board, developer not objecting/whistleblowing (★), management deploying.
  • The examples: ProPublica recidivism (mistaken high-risk more often for Black defendants) · GPT-3 / DALL·E (“all white male”) · Brandao 2019 pedestrian miss rates · Buolamwini & Gebru 2018 Gender Shades (up to 46.8% error on the darkest skin type against near-zero on the lightest).
  • Lived experience and diversity — the people finding these problems are disproportionately from the groups affected, which is an argument for diversity as error detection.

Part 4 is what to do about all of it.
