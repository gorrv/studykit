Mitigation & Responsibility

principles, governance, named methods — and who is answerable

Designing with ethical principles

Who publishes these
Many institutions are adopting guidelines for the ethical development and deployment of AI — governmental (UK EPSRC, NHS, EU…) and professional (IEEE, ACM…).
The seven principles table — reproduced in full
PrincipleDescription
Human oversight and agencyUpholding fundamental rights, informed decisions, correct trust, human-in-the-loop
Safety and robustnessMinimization of errors, functioning for a range of settings, resilience to attack
PrivacyConsent, anonymity, access, de-anonymization
TransparencyDocumentation and logging of data/decisions, explainability, informing when AI is used
FairnessNon-discrimination, accessibility, stakeholder participation
Societal & environmental wellbeingEnvironmental impact, impact to social relationships, democracy, politics
AccountabilityAuditability, documenting how dilemmas and trade-offs were resolved, responsibility for harms
The structure that makes this table memorable
The principles are the harms, restated as requirements. Line them up: agency ↔ human agency harms, safety ↔ safety and wellbeing, privacy ↔ privacy, fairness ↔ fairness and dignity, societal & environmental ↔ the last two harm categories, accountability ↔ accountability harms.

The one principle with no matching harm category is transparency — and the one harm category with no matching principle name is none, since societal and environmental are merged into one principle. So the mapping is nearly one-to-one, with transparency as the extra. If you can recall the seven harms you can reconstruct this table.

Note also what sits under accountability: documenting how dilemmas and trade-offs were resolved. Not avoiding trade-offs — recording how you made them. That is a realistic standard, and worth quoting.

Governance methods

Three items
  • Strengthening regulations, standards, codes of conduct — to safeguard current deployments and to guide/constrain the trajectory of future development.
  • Implementing processes of internal and external auditing, verification and validation.
  • “Trustworthy AI”: design AND governance that leads to AI we can trust — designing AI with ethical principles, and governing development and deployment.

The capitalised AND is the lecture's own emphasis, and it is the examinable point: “trustworthy AI” is not a design property alone. A perfectly principled system inside an ungoverned organisation is not trustworthy, and vice versa.

Two timescales in one bullet
Regulation is doing two different jobs: safeguarding what is already deployed (a present-tense job, backward-looking) and constraining the trajectory of future development (a forward-looking one). Most public argument about AI regulation conflates them, and separating them is a good move in a discussion answer.

Note too that auditing is specified as internal and external. Internal audit alone has an obvious conflict of interest, and external audit alone lacks access — the pairing is the point.

Value alignment methods

What it is
Aligning AI behaviour with human values.
  • Used extensively in chatbots.
  • For example, a machine learning model trained to predict how aligned a given output is to human values (e.g. honesty, clarity) based on many examples.
Both a technical and a social problem
  • How to encode behaviour rules into a system — the technical half.
  • Finding out what values we (and society) want for a system — the social half.

Important in Game AI, credit scoring, Gen AI — most systems.

The split is the thing to remember. Solving the technical half perfectly gets you a system that faithfully implements whatever values were specified — which, if the second half was skipped, is exactly the political-control slide from Part 1.

The third appearance of this idea
Value alignment was introduced in Topic 01 as the difficulty of exactly specifying “good”. Topics 4 and 8 made it concrete: the reward function is the specification, and an agent optimises precisely what you wrote, not what you meant. Here it returns as a named mitigation method. Being able to trace it through all three is the kind of cross-topic connection that reads well in an exam.

Bias mitigation methods

Four named methods — and they are genuinely different
MethodWhat it changesThe lecture's description
Dataset curationthe data itselfImprove the curation process, to appropriately represent minority and marginalized groups, to remove harmful content, etc. Includes participatory design.
Reweightingthe weight of each exampleDuring training, weight data from minority groups more highly.
Resamplinghow often each example is drawnDuring training, sample data from minority groups more often.
Fairness functionsthe objectiveInstead of maximizing average accuracy → maximize minimum accuracy; maximize accuracy and minimize performance differences, etc.

Reweighting and resampling are not the same method described twice. One changes each example's contribution to the loss; the other changes the composition of the batches. They often have similar effects, and an answer that treats them as synonyms is losing an easy mark.

Which method for which cause
The four map onto the five causes from Part 3 rather neatly, and pairing them is a strong way to answer a “how would you mitigate this” question:
causemethod that addresses it
Representation biasdataset curation · resampling · reweighting
Measurement biasnone of the four — you must change the label, or abandon the task
Algorithm biasfairness functions
Evaluation biasdisaggregated metrics — the “performance differences between social groups” row of the anticipation table
World biasfairness functions, or reconsidering the label — no data fix exists

Measurement bias is the one with no technical remedy on the list, which is why the phrenology slide ends the way it does.

Privacy mitigation methods

Two named methods
  • Anonymization algorithms — face blurring; text anonymization techniques.
  • Differential privacy — releasing information about a dataset in a way that does not allow you to know if a person is part of that dataset, usually by adding noise to computations in a smart way.
The distinction the definition is making
Anonymization removes identifying content. Differential privacy protects something stronger and less obvious: membership. Even a fully anonymised aggregate can leak the fact that a particular person was in the dataset — and if the dataset is “patients treated for X”, membership is the sensitive fact. That is why noise is added to the computation rather than to the records.

“In a smart way” is doing real work: the noise has to be calibrated so the aggregate stays useful while any single person's presence stays undetectable.

Environmental mitigation methods

Two named methods
  • Code emission estimation methods — many libraries exist, e.g. CodeCarbon.
  • Solving problems with smaller models.

Note the order: measure first, then reduce. It is the same shape as the anticipation table in Part 2 — you cannot manage what you have not instrumented.

Who is responsible for the harms of AI?

Three answers, and the lecture gives all three
WhoFor what
The institution that designs the systemFor allowing any of the harms to take place
The ethics boardFor passing an unethical product/application
The developers
  • Many harms are best anticipated by developers
  • Many harms can only be tested or minimized by developers
  • Responsible for algorithm transparency, inefficiency, power consumption…

The argument for developer responsibility is an argument from position, not from blame. It is not that developers are the worst actors — it is that they are frequently the only people who can see the problem coming and the only ones who can test for it. Responsibility follows capability. That is the reasoning to reproduce, and it is why four of the five bias causes carry the ★.

Minimising the harms of AI

The nine-item list
  1. Awareness of ethical issues; staying informed; being critical
  2. Participatory design
  3. Team diversity — marginalized communities have different lived experience, and it is important to anticipate issues
  4. Balanced datasets
  5. Testing with diverse groups
  6. Documenting datasets, models, algorithms, design choices
  7. Logging, explainable methods
  8. Ethics boards
  9. Whistleblowing
The list has a shape worth pointing out
It runs roughly through the lifecycle: before building (awareness, participatory design, team diversity) → while building (balanced datasets, testing with diverse groups) → documenting what you built (documentation, logging, explainability) → institutional checks (ethics boards) → and, when all of that fails, whistleblowing.

Whistleblowing being last is not an accident. It is the remedy for the second branch of the causes slide — the case where the risk was obvious, the task was problematic, and the organisation decided to proceed anyway. Every other item on the list assumes good faith; that one does not.

A “how would you minimise these harms” answer that names items from several stages will beat one that lists three technical fixes.

The conclusion slide — all five questions answered

Worth learning close to verbatim
QuestionThe lecture's answer
How is AI applied in practice? Who has access? Who benefits? Well-meaning and bad applications; unequal access; unequal benefits
What are the harms and ethical considerations of AI? Who is harmed? Human agency, safety, privacy, transparency, fairness, societal, environmental, accountability; more harm often to already marginalized groups
How does bias manifest in AI? Why does it arise? Different impact on different social groups; because of world / representation / measurement / algorithm / evaluation bias, and lack of anticipation, testing and action
Who is responsible for the harms of AI? Institutions designing algorithms, managers, ethics boards, and developers too
How do we minimise these harms? Awareness, anticipation, participation, diversity, testing, documentation, reporting, whistleblowing

Two things to notice. The harms line in the conclusion says “transparency” where the harms slide said fairness and dignity — the conclusion is loosely blending the harm list with the principles list, so quote whichever the question asks for. And “more harm often to already marginalized groups” is the single sentence that ties the entire lecture together: it is the answer to “who is harmed?” in every section.

Exam takeaways

What to have ready
  • The seven principles: human oversight and agency · safety and robustness · privacy · transparency · fairness · societal & environmental wellbeing · accountability. They mirror the seven harms, with transparency as the extra.
  • Governance: regulations/standards/codes (safeguard present, constrain future) · internal and external auditing, verification, validation · “Trustworthy AI” = design AND governance.
  • Value alignment is technical (encode the rules) and social (decide what the values are).
  • Bias mitigation: dataset curation (incl. participatory design) · reweighting · resampling · fairness functions. Know that reweighting ≠ resampling, and that fairness functions maximise the minimum accuracy.
  • Privacy mitigation: anonymization (face blurring, text) · differential privacy (hides membership, by adding noise to computations).
  • Environmental mitigation: emission estimation (e.g. CodeCarbon) · smaller models.
  • Responsibility: the institution, the ethics board, and developers — the last because they are best placed to anticipate and the only ones able to test.
  • Minimising: awareness · participatory design · team diversity · balanced datasets · testing with diverse groups · documentation · logging and explainability · ethics boards · whistleblowing.
  • The conclusion slide answers all five key questions in one line each — the safest possible structure for an open-ended question.
