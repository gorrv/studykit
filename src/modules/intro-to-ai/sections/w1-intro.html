What is Artificial Intelligence?

definitions, approaches, and the rational agent

Two senses of the phrase

The distinction the lecture opens with
  1. AI is a kind of intelligence — artificial, created by humans.
    • You can use (artificial) intelligence to solve a problem.
    • Some thing or behaviour can be (artificially) intelligent.
    • But there is NO such thing as using "an AI" / "an intelligence".
  2. AI is a science / field of study / field of research — with the goal of creating intelligent systems.
Why the slide bothers making this point
"An AI" treats intelligence as a countable object, like a gadget. The lecture insists it's either a property something has, or a field that studies how to give things that property. Worth using the careful phrasing in written answers — it signals you understood the framing.
The founding definition — John McCarthy, 1956
"AI is the science and engineering of making intelligent machines, especially intelligent computer programs. It is related to the similar task of using computers to understand human intelligence, but AI does not have to confine itself to methods that are biologically observable."

The last clause is the load-bearing one: an intelligent machine does not have to work the way a brain works. That single sentence is what licenses the "rational" column in the table below.

Four approaches to AI

If AI is "the science of making intelligent machines" — what counts as an intelligent machine? There are two independent choices, giving four approaches:

Like humans

ThinkSystems that think like humansSystems that think rationally

Reading the two axes
Think vs act — is the machine judged on its internal reasoning, or only on its behaviour?
Humanly vs rationally — is the benchmark what a person would do, or what is provably the best thing to do?

These give very different research programmes. Copying humans means copying our mistakes too; being rational means possibly behaving in ways no person would. This module is almost entirely in the act rationally box.

Systems that act like humans

The criterion
A system is intelligent if a person cannot distinguish its behaviour from a human's.

Turing test

Proposed by Alan Turing in 1950.

  • Let a human ask questions to a system.
  • If the human cannot tell whether the answers came from a human or a machine, it passes the test.

Total Turing test

  • A robot is intelligent if a person cannot distinguish its behaviour from a human's.
  • Includes physical behaviour — not just conversation.

Systems that act rationally: agents

Three definitions to know exactly
  • Agent — a unit that makes an intelligent decision when given the opportunity. It uses the knowledge it has at that point in time to make the decision, and the decision is limited by the range of actions possible at that point in time.
  • Autonomous agent — makes decisions without human input.
  • Rational agent — always takes the best action at a given point.

Examples: a robot, an autonomous vehicle, an enemy monster in a game.

the agent loop, built up over three slides
Perception comes in through sensors; knowledge feeds each stage; the decision goes out through effectors as an action.

Two ways of encoding knowledge

The knowledge box on the left — how the world evolves, effects of my actions, goals — can be filled in two very different ways. This is the split that divides the whole field.

Model-based

explicit knowledge → Symbolic / Classical AI

  • Define an encoding of what the world looks like.
  • Define how we can change that world.
  • Search for good strategies on how to change the world the way we want to.
  • Pretends to think like a human.

Data-driven

tacit knowledge → Machine Learning

  • Reads in coarse data from a specific problem.
  • Is told the problem it's trying to solve.
  • Changes the model such that it gets the correct output.
  • Pretends to learn like a human.
Note the third bullet on the left — that's this whole module
"Search for good strategies on how to change the world the way we want to" is a one-line description of search, which is Part 2. Classical AI encodes the world explicitly and then searches; machine learning fits a model to data instead. Also note the deliberate parallel in the last bullets: one pretends to think like a human, the other pretends to learn like one.

Why this table matters more than it looks
Every technique in the module assumes a particular set of answers. The search algorithms in Parts 2 and 3 all assume the easy end of every axis: perfect perception, a fully observable static environment, and instantaneous deterministic actions. If a question describes a problem that breaks one of those assumptions, that's the hint that plain search won't do.

The point of the two quotes
Simon said chess in ten years; it took 39. Minsky said the whole problem in a generation; the lecture's own summary is that many open challenges still exist and true general intelligence is still a long way off. The pairing is there to show how badly the field has historically mis-estimated difficulty.

The value alignment problem

Definition
  • How to align the values of your system with those of the humans who use, and are impacted by, the system?
  • How to exactly specify what a "good" song recommendation / conversation / game experience is?
Connect it back to the agent diagram
Look at the knowledge box: one of the three things in it is goals. A rational agent "always takes the best action" — but best according to what? The value alignment problem is precisely the difficulty of filling in that one box correctly. The agent will optimise exactly what you specify, which is only useful if what you specified is what you meant.

Human vs artificial intelligence

Human

  • Self-directed goals
  • Common sense
  • Learn from few examples
  • Adapt to similar but different tasks
  • Emotional intelligence
  • Humans ask questions!

Artificial

  • Statistical reasoning
  • Large-scale maths
  • Huge state-space exploration
  • Computational power (at scale)
"Huge state-space exploration"
That's the entry in the right-hand column that Parts 2 and 3 are about. The machine's advantage isn't insight — it's the ability to examine enormous numbers of possibilities systematically. Search is how that advantage gets used.

Exam-style takeaways

Key things to nail for Part 1
  1. The two senses of AI — a kind of intelligence (a property, not a countable object — "no such thing as an AI") and a field of research aiming to create intelligent systems.
  2. McCarthy's 1956 definition, especially the closing clause: AI does not have to confine itself to biologically observable methods.
  3. The four approaches — think/act × like humans/rationally. Know that "like humans" means cognitive models and "rationally" means laws from logic and probability.
  4. Turing test (1950, question-and-answer) vs total Turing test (a robot, including physical behaviour).
  5. Agent / autonomous agent / rational agent — be able to give all three definitions, including that an agent's decision is limited by the knowledge and the actions available at that point in time.
  6. Draw the agent loop — sensors → what the world is like now → what it will be like if I do A → what action to do now → effectors, with knowledge (how the world evolves, effects of my actions, goals) feeding in.
  7. Model-based vs data-driven — explicit/symbolic/classical vs tacit/machine learning, four bullets each, and the "pretends to think" / "pretends to learn" contrast.
  8. The seven dimensions — explicit/tacit, model-based/data-driven, perfect/imperfect perception, fully/partially observable, static/dynamic, instantaneous/durative, deterministic/stochastic.
  9. The history dates — 1950 Turing, 1956 McCarthy, 1958 Simon's prediction, 1967 Minsky's prediction, 1997 Deep Blue, 2016 AlphaGo, 2017 AlphaZero, 2019 AlphaStar.
  10. Value alignment — aligning system values with human ones, and the difficulty of exactly specifying what "good" means.
  11. Human vs AI strengths — the two lists, and note humans ask questions.
