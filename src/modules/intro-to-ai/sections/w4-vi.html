Value Iteration

guess zeros, sweep until still, read off the arrows

The Bellman update

The equation
Ui+1(s)  =  R(s)  +  γ  maxa  Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Ui(s′)

The Σ part is exactly E[U | s, a] from Part 3. Nothing new has appeared — the update just wraps that expected value in two more steps.

Read it in four pieces, right to left
piecewhat it doeswho is in charge
Σs′ P(s′|s,a) Ui(s′)the expected value of one actionthe world — so average over what it might do
maxatry every action, keep the best numberyou — so take the best
γshrink it, if the future is worth less than nowa setting
R(s)add the fee for standing herethe problem

As a sentence: the new value of a square is what it pays you now, plus the best you can expect to get from here.

One line to remember
Max over your options, average over the world's.

The max is the agent. Take it away and there is nobody choosing — the world just drifts, and you are back to a Markov chain. That single symbol is the difference between watching a process and making decisions.

And notice the subscripts: the new values Ui+1 are computed from the old values Ui. You do a whole sweep using last round's numbers, then swap them in.

The algorithm

Four steps
  1. Set every non-terminal square to 0. Terminals keep their +1 and −1 forever — they are given, not guessed.
  2. Sweep: apply the Bellman update to every square, using the previous round's values.
  3. Measure δ — the largest amount any single square changed this sweep.
  4. Stop when δ is small enough. Otherwise go back to step 2.

That is the entire algorithm. There is no cleverness in it — the cleverness is that repeating a local rule enough times solves a global problem.

Why starting at 0 is not cheating
The starting values are wrong and it does not matter. Each sweep pulls every square a little closer to the truth, and the terminals anchor the whole thing because they were never a guess. Start from any numbers you like and you converge to the same answer.

Why U₁ is almost entirely −0.04

Every square looked at its neighbours. They were all still 0. So the expected value of every action was 0, the max of a set of zeros is 0, and the square got

−0.04 + 1 × 0 = −0.04

One square is different. (3,3) touches the +1, so one of its actions could actually see a non-zero number:

U₁(3,3) = −0.04 + 1 × [ 0.8×1 + 0.1×0 + 0.1×0 ] = 0.76
And (3,2) touches the −1 — so why is it still −0.04?
Because its best action simply avoids the pit, and avoiding costs nothing yet. Its best move is Left into the wall: you stay put, expected value 0. Moving Up would risk the −1 for no gain, so the max never picks it.

Good news changes a square's value immediately. Bad news does not — not until it starts closing off good routes. That happens later, once the surrounding squares are worth something and dodging the pit finally means giving something up.

This trips people up when they check their hand-worked table and think they have made a mistake. They have not.

How information travels
Utility spreads outwards from the terminals, one square per sweep. After one sweep only (3,3) has heard about the +1. After two, its neighbours have. And so on.

That is why (4,1) is the last to settle — it is furthest from anything good — and why the number of sweeps grows with the size of the world. Value iteration is a flood fill, carrying value backwards from the rewards. Watch it happen here.

A note on the lecture slide's U₁ map
The slide shows −0.02 in the U₁ cells and 0.60 at (3,3). But its converged map shows 0.812 / 0.868 / 0.918, which are the published values for R(s) = −0.04, γ = 1.

Those two maps cannot come from the same parameters. The U₁ picture appears to have been drawn with a different reward or discount. The numbers above are what you get from the parameters the rest of the deck uses, and the solver below reproduces them exactly.

If an exam asks for one iteration by hand: state your R and γ, and show the arithmetic. That is where the marks are.

When to stop

The figure

Recomputed from the same grid. (3,3) shoots up almost immediately. The far squares — (1,1), (3,1), (4,1) — start negative, because at first all they know about is the step fee piling up, and only climb once the good news reaches them. Everything has settled by about iteration 25, with (4,1) lowest at 0.388.

The stopping rule
stop when   δ  <  ε(1 − γ) / γ
symbolis
δ (delta)the biggest change any square made this sweep
ε (epsilon)how much error you are willing to accept in the final answer
γ (gamma)the discount factor

In practice: stop when the numbers have stopped moving. The formula just makes "stopped moving" precise, and ties it to how wrong you are prepared to be.

Two things worth understanding rather than memorising

Why large γ is expensive. γ controls how far ahead the algorithm must look. At γ = 0.5, a reward ten steps away is worth 0.510 ≈ 0.001 — invisible, so a few sweeps are enough. At γ = 0.99 nothing fades, so information has to cross the entire grid before anything settles.

Why the curves dip negative first. Early on, a distant square has only seen step fees piling up and has no idea a +1 exists. It genuinely looks like a bad place to be. Only when the good news arrives does the estimate climb. Intermediate values are not meaningful — only the final fixpoint is.

Getting the policy out

The utilities are not the answer you wanted
Value iteration gives you a number per square. What you actually wanted was an action per square. One more pass converts one into the other:
π(s) = arg maxa  Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) U(s′)

In words: at each square, work out the expected value of every action one last time — and keep the action that scored highest, instead of the score.

max vs arg max — the only difference between the two equations
maxarg max
Returnsthe best valuethe action that achieved it
Used byvalue iteration — it needs a number to keep iteratingpolicy extraction — it needs behaviour
Examplemax = 0.8arg max = Right
Why R(s) and γ vanished from the policy formula
Neither can change which action wins. R(s) is the same whatever you choose — it is the fee for the square you are already standing on. And γ multiplies every action's score by the same amount, so it cannot reorder them.

So the policy is a single arg max over the converged utilities — one cheap pass at the very end, after all the iterating is done.

An honest note about the γ blow-up
Figure 17.5(b) shows iterations rocketing to 105 and beyond as γ → 1. On this 11-state grid you will not see that: with the same stopping rule the count only goes from about 9 sweeps at γ = 0.5 to 31 at γ = 0.999.

The shape of the curve is right and the cause is right — the threshold ε(1−γ)/γ tightens towards zero while convergence slows to rate γ — but the magnitude in the figure reflects a general worst-case bound, not this particular tiny world. Value spreads across 11 squares in a handful of sweeps no matter what γ is. Quote the trend in an exam, not a number.

Exam-style takeaways

Key things to nail for Part 4
  1. The Bellman update — Ui+1(s) = R(s) + γ maxa Σs′ P(s′|s,a) Ui(s′). Write it from memory.
  2. Max over your actions, average over the world's outcomes.
  3. Initialisation — U(s) = 0 everywhere except end states, where U(s) = R(s).
  4. Stop when Ui+1(s) = Ui(s) for all s; formally when δ < ε(1−γ)/γ, where δ is the largest change in any state this sweep and ε the error you'll tolerate.
  5. Be able to do one iteration by hand. On the standard grid with R = −0.04, γ = 1: everything is −0.04 after one sweep except (3,3) = −0.04 + 0.8 = 0.76.
  6. The converged utilities — 0.812, 0.868, 0.918 / 0.762, wall, 0.660 / 0.705, 0.655, 0.611, 0.388.
  7. Extracting the policy — π(s) = arg maxa Σs′ P(s′|s,a) U(s′). Note R(s) and γ drop out.
  8. max vs arg max — one returns the value, the other the action.
  9. Convergence behaviour — distant states dip negative before climbing; ~20–30 iterations on this grid; iterations blow up as γ → 1, and depend only weakly on the error tolerance.
  10. Utility spreads outwards from the terminals, roughly one square per iteration.
  11. The policy stabilises before the utilities do — worth a sentence if you're asked when you can stop.
