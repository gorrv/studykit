Underfitting & Overfitting

the two ways a model goes wrong

Generalisation error

The definition, and the problem with it
  • When we develop a model, we want to minimise the error it will have on unseen data. This is, informally, the generalisation error.
  • We rarely have a model for the unseen data, and we only have samples to deal with. We can, then, minimise the empirical error — the one we have control of.
The gap between those two sentences is this entire topic
The thing you care about is not the thing you can measure. You want low error on data that does not exist yet; all you can compute is error on the data in front of you. So you minimise the empirical error and hope it stands in for the generalisation error.

Underfitting and overfitting are the two ways that hope fails:

  • Underfitting — empirical error is high, so generalisation error is high too. At least it's honest about it.
  • Overfitting — empirical error is tiny and generalisation error is enormous. The measurement actively lies to you, which is what makes it the dangerous one.

Underfitting

Definition
Underfitting is when the model fails to capture the complexity of the (training) data — or fails to find a pattern in the data.

The model is too simple.

Overfitting

Definition
Overfitting is when the model fits too much to the training data and fails to generalise to unseen data.

The model is too complex.

The mental image
The data is a true underlying pattern plus noise. A model that is too simple can't even follow the pattern. A model that is too complex follows the noise as well — and the noise is different next time, so everything it learned about it is worthless.

Overfitting is memorisation. A model with enough parameters can pass exactly through every training point and score zero training error while knowing nothing at all — which is precisely why the test set has to be untouched.

The three plots

The standard demonstration fits polynomials of increasing degree to the same noisy sample of a smooth curve:

The same three fits, drawn

Dots are the 30 data points. The dashed grey line is the true smooth curve they were sampled from — the model never sees it. The blue line is what each polynomial actually fitted.

Notice the blue line vanishing off the top and bottom of the third panel. Degree 29 passes through every dot exactly, and to do that it swings out to roughly +234 and −15 in the gaps between them. Any new point landing in one of those gaps is missed by an enormous margin, which is the entire content of that 1.76e+15.

"Degree" is simply how bendy the curve is allowed to be: degree 1 cannot bend at all, degree 4 bends a few times, degree 29 can bend 28 times. (Figures recomputed here from 30 samples of cos(1.5πx) with noise; the exact numbers depend on the sample, the pattern never does.)

Read the two columns separately — they tell opposite stories
Training MSE falls the whole way: 0.189 → 0.0069 → 0.0032. By that number alone, degree 29 is the best model of the three.

Validation MSE does not: 0.379 → 0.012 → 1,760,000,000,000,000. Degree 29 is worse than a straight line by roughly fifteen orders of magnitude.

That is the single most important pair of rows in the topic. Training error can only ever go down as you add complexity — a more flexible model can always reproduce whatever a simpler one did. So training error can never tell you when to stop. Only held-out error can.

Where a number like 1015 even comes from
A degree-29 polynomial through ~30 points has enough freedom to pass through every single one exactly — and to do it, its coefficients become gigantic and of alternating sign. Between the points it therefore swings wildly, shooting to ±107 in the gaps. Square that and average, and you get 1015.

You can see it in the third plot: the fitted curve is a set of near-vertical spikes. It is perfect at the training points and insane everywhere between them.

Increasing model complexity

The figures
In this example, the degree of the polynomial can be understood as our model complexity. We would choose in this case the lower validation error, so degree of 4.

Plotting both errors against degree gives the shape you should be able to draw from memory:

Training error falls and flattens. Validation error is U-shaped. Pick the bottom of the U.
The three things to say about this curve
  1. Training error is monotonically decreasing (then flat). More complexity can never fit the training data worse.
  2. Validation error is U-shaped. Left of the minimum you are underfitting; right of it you are overfitting.
  3. Choose the complexity at the minimum of the validation curve — here, degree 4.

A fourth, worth a mark: the widening gap between the two curves is itself the diagnosis. Both high and close together = underfitting. Training near zero while validation climbs = overfitting.

"Why validation and not test?"

The closing question — answer it properly
Because the moment you use a set to make a decision, it stops being untouched — and an untouched set was the entire point of having one.

If you try all eight degrees and pick the one with the lowest test error, you have fitted your choice of model to the test set. Its error is now an optimistic estimate of generalisation error: you selected it precisely because it happened to do well there, noise included. Report that number and you are quoting a best-of-eight, not an honest estimate.

So the data splits three ways
SetUsed forSeen by
Trainingfitting the model's parametersthe learning algorithm
Validationchoosing between models — the degree, the hyperparametersyou, the person choosing
Testthe final honest estimate of generalisation errornothing, until the very end
Note the vocabulary shift — it is deliberate
Part 2 defined only training and test. Part 3's plots are all labelled "Validation MSE". That is not an inconsistency: as soon as the polynomial degree became something to choose, a set was needed to choose it with, and the test set could not be spent on that.

The rule in one line: train on the training set, choose on the validation set, report on the test set — once.

  • Push the degree towards the number of training points and watch the fitted curve start spiking between the data while training error keeps falling.
  • Look at the sweep table: the training column never rises; the validation column is U-shaped. The tool marks the minimum for you — that is the degree you would choose.
  • Drop the training points to 8 and look at degree 7: training MSE 5.9e−22 — the polynomial passes exactly through all eight points, because 8 coefficients can always fit 8 points — while validation MSE is 4.3e+02. That is overfitting in its purest possible form. Everything above degree 7 is reported as singular: more coefficients than data, and the fit is not even defined.
  • Set noise to 0 and the U disappears — validation error just keeps falling, all the way to 1.9e−16 at degree 13. With no noise there is nothing to memorise, so extra complexity costs nothing. Overfitting is a disease of noisy data, not of complexity by itself.
  • Change the seed to redraw the sample. The exact numbers move and the ★ may land on a neighbouring degree — the shape never changes. I checked 40 seeds: training error was monotonically non-increasing in all 40, and the validation minimum was strictly interior (a real U) in all 40.

Exam-style takeaways

Key things to nail for Part 3
  1. Generalisation error = the error the model will have on unseen data. We can't compute it, so we minimise the empirical error, the one we have control of.
  2. Underfitting = the model fails to capture the complexity of the training data. Too simple.
  3. Overfitting = the model fits too much to the training data and fails to generalise. Too complex.
  4. Be able to draw the two curves — training error monotonically decreasing then flat; validation error U-shaped.
  5. Choose the complexity at the minimum of the validation curve — in the worked example, degree 4.
  6. Know the three sets of numbers — degree 1: train 1.89e−01, validation 3.79e−01. Degree 4: 6.91e−03, 1.20e−02. Degree 29: 3.22e−03, 1.76e+15.
  7. Training error alone can never tell you when to stop, because it only ever goes down as complexity rises.
  8. Diagnose from the gap — both errors high and close = underfitting; training near zero and validation climbing = overfitting.
  9. "Why validation and not test?" — because choosing with a set means fitting to it, and the test error would then be an optimistic estimate rather than an honest one. Train / choose / report, on three separate sets.
