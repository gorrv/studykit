The Seven Harms

a taxonomy worth knowing in order

The seven categories

The list, in the standard order
  1. Human agency harms
  2. Accountability harms
  3. Safety and wellbeing harms
  4. Privacy harms
  5. Fairness and dignity harms
  6. Societal harms
  7. Environmental harms

A question that says “discuss the harms of this system” is asking you to walk this list. Most systems touch four or five of them, and naming the categories you rule out is worth as much as developing the ones you keep.

1 · Human agency

Four items
  • Loss of human control over algorithms
  • Loss of the human right to contest decisions
  • Over-delegation
  • Loss of critical thinking

All four can occur while the system is working perfectly. That is what distinguishes agency harms: they are about what happens to the human in the loop, not about errors.

2 · Accountability

Two items, pointing in opposite directions
  • Loss of human accountability for harm — nobody is answerable.
  • Incorrect blaming of humans for AI failures — the wrong person is answerable. The lecture's example: warehouse workers blamed for robot mistakes.

Worth stating as a pair in an answer. Accountability can fail by evaporating or by landing on the wrong person, and the second is easy to forget.

3 · Safety and wellbeing

Four items
  • Physical safety
  • Abuse and tech-facilitated violence
  • Health
  • Wellbeing

The lecture gives physical safety its own slide first and then adds the other three, which is a hint about how easily the non-physical ones get overlooked. Abuse and tech-facilitated violence sits here rather than under privacy, because the harm is the abuse the tool enables, not the information it exposes.

4 · Privacy invasion

Split by setting, not by mechanism
  • Privacy in intimate spaces
  • Privacy at work & school
  • Privacy in public spaces

Name the setting in an answer. “Privacy in public spaces” is the least intuitive of the three and the one that covers city-scale camera networks — the expectation of privacy does not vanish because you are outdoors.

Why the split matters practically
Allocative harms are measurable and representational harms often are not. You can count how many loans went to each group; you cannot straightforwardly count how demeaned a depiction is. That asymmetry is why the anticipation slide pairs quantitative methods with qualitative methods and critique — some harms are only reachable through interviews, ethnography and argument.

Quality-of-service is the sub-type students most often miss. Nobody is refused the service; it simply works worse for them. Gender Shades and the pedestrian-detection results in Part 3 are both this.

6 · Societal

Five items
  • Information
  • Cultural
  • Civic and political
  • Social relationships
  • Deskilling

Societal harms have no identifiable individual victim — that is the test for putting something here. A polarised electorate and a deskilled profession are harms to a public, which is also why they are the hardest to litigate and the easiest to ignore.

Deskilling vs over-delegation — a distinction worth making
Over-delegation (human agency) is about this decision, now being handed to a system. Deskilling (societal) is about the capability being lost across a profession, so that nobody could take the decision back even if they wanted to. One is a choice; the other is the accumulated consequence of everyone making it.

7 · Environmental

Three items
  • Energy consumption and pollution
  • Mineral mining for datacentres
  • AI and robots for the oil & gas industry

The third is the one people forget, and it is different in kind from the other two: the harm is not the model's own footprint but what the model is used to accelerate. A perfectly efficient model that helps locate new oil reserves is an environmental harm by this taxonomy.

What happens after harm — reparation

Ten types of harm reparation
  1. Public outcry — protests, petitions, online backlash
  2. Perpetrator communication — denials, justifications, apologies
  3. Investigation of harm — audits, journalism
  4. Initiation of legal actions
  5. Legal repercussions — fines, criminal charges, dismissals
  6. Compensation — refunds, settlements
  7. Removal — data removal, product recall
  8. Product discontinuation
  9. Design changes
  10. Law/policy change
Reading the list as a sequence
It is roughly ordered from immediate and informal to slow and structural. Outcry costs nothing and changes nothing on its own; law change is the only item that affects anyone other than the perpetrator. Note that “perpetrator communication” includes denials and justifications — the list is descriptive of what does happen, not prescriptive of what should.

All ten are after the harm. That is precisely why the next slide exists.

Anticipating and evaluating harm — before it happens

Four families of method
FamilyMethods
Quantitative measurement
of model/data statistics
  • Performance differences between social groups (e.g. accuracy)
  • Output differences between social groups
  • Incidence of bad data (e.g. explicit content)
Ablation studies
  • Removing/changing data and observing results
  • Changing algorithm parameters and observing results
Qualitative methods Interviews · Surveys · Workshops · Literature reviews · Ethnography
Critique Speculation · Logical or philosophical argument
Why all four families are needed
Quantitative methods can only find harms you already thought to measure. A performance gap between groups shows up in a table only once you have decided which groups to break the data down by — and deciding that is not itself a quantitative act.

That is what qualitative methods and critique are for, and it is also why “who is discovering these issues” gets its own slide in Part 3. A question asking how you would evaluate a system for harm should name methods from more than one family.

Note too that “performance differences between social groups” is precisely the disaggregated evaluation that the fairness tool computes — the first row of this table is a thing you now know how to do.

Exam takeaways

What to have ready
  • The seven harms in order: human agency · accountability · safety and wellbeing · privacy · fairness and dignity · societal · environmental.
  • Allocative vs representational is the key sub-distinction. Allocative = opportunities, resources, quality-of-service. Representational = stereotyping, demeaning, erasing, alienating, loss of self-identification, essentialist categories.
  • Accountability fails two ways: it evaporates, or it lands on the wrong person.
  • Privacy is split by setting: intimate spaces, work & school, public spaces.
  • Societal harms have no identifiable individual victim — that is the test.
  • Environmental includes AI for oil & gas, not just the model's own footprint.
  • Reparation happens after the harm — ten types, from public outcry to law change. Anticipation happens before — quantitative, ablation, qualitative, critique.
  • Deskilling (societal) ≠ over-delegation (agency); abuse (safety) ≠ privacy invasion.
