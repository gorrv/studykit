Topic 01 · What Security Means

Nothing Here Is Secret Yet

syntax, correctness, and five attempts at saying what a cipher should do

This topic contains no clever cipher and almost no arithmetic. It is about the sentence that has to come before either: what are we claiming, exactly, when we say a scheme is secure? Most of the work in the subject turns out to be in pinning that down, and the ciphers follow from it. Below, four attempts at the sentence get written and knocked down in turn, each by a scheme that satisfies the previous attempt and is still obviously useless — and each attack is run on the page rather than described.

What the subject is about

Several definitions, none of them agreeing
A dictionarythe art or practice of writing in code or cipher; the science of encryption.
Katz–Lindellthe study of mathematical techniques for securing digital information, systems and distributed computations against adversarial attacks.
Rosulekthe study of controlling access to information.
Martinto study the limits of computing.
Blanchettecode created with the sole purpose of regulating behaviour.
The dictionary one is the weakest, and it is the one most people start with. Encryption is a part of cryptography roughly as sorting is a part of computer science.
The disagreement is the point
These are not competing attempts at one fact. They are five different views of what the subject is for: a technique, a science of limits, a way of controlling access, a means of regulating what people can do. Which definition you reach for changes which questions look important — and the last one, in particular, is a reminder that a cryptographic design is a decision about who gets to do what.

Two lords and a rider

The setting everything else is built on
Two lords, Francis and Henry, want to communicate securely between their pavilions. Meeting in person, they agree on three things: a method of encryption that turns a message into a ciphertext using a secret key, a method of decryption that reverses it using the same key, and the key itself, whispered so that nobody overhears.

To send a message, Francis encrypts it, writes the ciphertext on parchment, and hands it to his most trusted rider. Henry decrypts it and reads it.
Now ask what can go wrong
  • The scheme. What if the lords are not very clever and the cipher is bad? What would "bad" even mean?
  • The parchment. Can it be altered, added to, shortened, or its parts reordered — and still decrypt to something that reads plausibly?
  • The rider. Can they be captured, bribed, threatened, or simply made to arrive late?
Three questions, and they turn out to need three different kinds of answer. The first is secrecy, the second is integrity, and the third is largely not cryptography at all.

Secret key encryption, as syntax only

SKE = (KGen, Enc, Dec)
A secret key encryption scheme is a triple of algorithms, over a key space K, a message space M and a ciphertext space C:
KGenoutputs a secret key sk from K   sk ← KGen
Enctakes sk and a message m from M, outputs a ciphertext c from C   c = Enc(sk, m)
Dectakes sk and a ciphertext c, outputs a message   m = Dec(sk, c)

This says nothing whatever about correctness or security. It is the shape of the thing: what goes in, what comes out. The arrow in sk ← KGen rather than = is doing a job — key generation is allowed to be random, and had better be.

Correctness, which is also not security

The one obligation that is not about adversaries
If a secret key is used to encrypt a message into a ciphertext, then decrypting that ciphertext with the same key returns the same message. 
c = Enc(sk, m)   ⇒   m = Dec(sk, c)
That is all correctness asks, and a scheme can satisfy it perfectly while being completely useless. The tool below checks correctness by running it over an entire domain — and then breaks the scheme anyway.

Five attempts at "a good encryption scheme"

Each attempt below is a reasonable-sounding sentence. Each is then killed by a scheme that satisfies it and is plainly no good — which is how you can tell the sentence was not yet saying what you meant.

i

You cannot recover the secret key by observing ciphertexts.

Killed by: the scheme that ignores the key and writes the message out as the ciphertext. The key is beyond recovery because nothing ever touches it.

ii

You cannot recover messages by observing ciphertexts.

Killed by: a scheme that leaks the first letter, or the first word. "The message" was not recovered, and the damage is done anyway.

iii

You cannot recover even partial messages by observing ciphertexts.

Killed by: anything that leaks statistics. Knowing how often each letter occurs is not a part of the message, and it is enough to break a shift cipher outright.

iv

You cannot infer any further meaningful or new information about messages by observing ciphertexts.

Getting warmer. Not yet a formal statement — "meaningful" and "infer" are carrying a great deal — but the intent is now right. What if the adversary can do more than observe?

v

You cannot infer any further meaningful or new information about messages by observing ciphertexts, even given the ability to encrypt and decrypt messages and ciphertexts of your choice.

This captures the adversaries we expect to meet. Whether it is realistic to hand an attacker a decryption box is a fair question — and the answer is that assuming too much of an adversary costs you very little, while assuming too little costs you everything.

What that exercise was really teaching
Every one of those sentences has two halves, and a definition is only as good as both:
What must not be possibleinferring further meaningful information about the messages.
Given what capabilitiesobserving ciphertexts — or, in addition, encrypting and decrypting chosen inputs.

Change either half and you have a different definition. A scheme is never "secure" on its own; it is secure against something, for something. This family of properties usually travels under the name confidentiality, or sometimes secrecy.

The one scheme that survives rung iv completely

The one-time pad
Take one letter of key per letter of message, each chosen uniformly at random, and add them letter by letter. Decryption subtracts the same pad.

An eavesdropper who sees the ciphertext learns nothing — not "nothing practical", not "nothing without enormous computation", but nothing at all. The tool below counts out why, over every message and every ciphertext of a given length.

And yet it is not the end of the story
Perfect secrecy is the strongest possible answer to rung iv, and the pad is still not a scheme you would deploy. The key is as long as the message and may never be reused, so the problem of sending a secret message has been swapped for the problem of sending an equally long secret key. Worse — and this is the subject of the rest of this topic — secrecy was never the only thing being asked for.

Security that is not secrecy

The rider is bribed
Suppose the rider is paid to alter the parchment. They do not know the key and cannot read the message. They can still reorder characters, add some, or remove some.

Henry decrypts and gets — what? If he is lucky, gibberish, and he notices. If he is not, something that reads perfectly well and is not what Francis wrote. The assurance that what arrived is what was sent is a separate security property, and it has its own name: integrity.
Secrecy does not imply integrity, and the pad proves it
It is tempting to assume that a message nobody can read is a message nobody can meddle with. The one-time pad — the most secret scheme there is — is the cleanest counterexample. Add a letter to the ciphertext and the same letter is added to what Henry reads. The attacker never learns the message and never touches the key, and still decides what the message becomes.

How Francis could fix it
Two quite different kinds of answer, and it is worth seeing that they are different:
  • Engineering. Indelible ink, fill every empty space on the parchment so nothing can be inserted, tamper-evident seals.
  • Cryptography. Compute a tag from the ciphertext and a second shared key, write it beside the ciphertext, and have Henry check the tag before he decrypts anything.

Message authentication codes, as syntax only

MAC = (KGen, Tag, Vf)
KGenoutputs an integrity key ik from a key space K   ik ← KGen
Tagtakes ik and a message m, outputs a tag τ from a tag space T   τ = Tag(ik, m)
Vftakes ik, a message and a tag, and outputs 0 or 1   1 = Vf(ik, m, τ)

Once again: syntax only. Nothing here says what makes a MAC a good MAC, and the same ladder of attempts would have to be climbed again to say it. Note also that this is a second key — secrecy and integrity are achieved by different means, and generally with different keys.

Still more properties

Things a rider can do that neither property covers
usually counted as cryptography
  • reordering ciphertexts and tags
  • repeating them
  • reflecting them — sending them back the way they came
usually counted as network engineering
  • delaying delivery
  • denying delivery altogether
A valid, correctly tagged ciphertext, delivered twice, is a problem that neither secrecy nor integrity as stated above says anything about. Each of these needs its own sentence, with its own two halves.

Alice, Bob, and the people trying to read their post

The usual names
The two machines holding the keys are conventionally Alice and Bob rather than two lords. The adversary gets a name according to what they are allowed to do: Eve eavesdrops and nothing more; Charlie is active — he can have messages of his choice encrypted and ciphertexts of his choice decrypted.

Those two names are rungs iv and v wearing a disguise. Eve is rarely a strong enough adversary to design against.
Why bother with all this formality
  • It states the adversary's capabilities explicitly, instead of leaving them to be assumed differently by everyone reading.
  • It gives a shared meaning, rather than relying on how two people each happen to read an English sentence.
  • It lets us be pessimistic on purpose. Assume the adversary is stronger than they are likely to be, and the result applies in more situations than the one you had in mind.
An aside: cryptography as a munition
Until the late 1990s, the United States classed cryptography as a munition, which made exporting its source code illegal. Early versions of the Netscape browser therefore shipped in two flavours: a domestic one with full-length keys, and an international one with deliberately weakened ones. The author of PGP was investigated after its source code left the country; the legal argument that followed turned on whether source code is speech. The courts eventually decided that it is.

Takeaways

If you remember six things
  1. SKE is a triple (KGen, Enc, Dec) over spaces K, M, C — and stating it says nothing about correctness or security.
  2. Correctness is Dec(sk, Enc(sk, m)) = m, and the "do nothing" cipher satisfies it perfectly.
  3. A security definition has two halves: what must not be possible, and what the adversary is allowed to do. Only stating both says anything.
  4. Each rung of the ladder is killed by a scheme that satisfies the previous one — the key is safe because it is unused; the message is not recovered but its first letter is; no letter is recovered but all the statistics are.
  5. Secrecy and integrity are different properties achieved by different means, and the one-time pad proves the first does not imply the second.
  6. MAC is a triple (KGen, Tag, Vf) with a separate key, and tags are checked before decrypting.
Things that catch people out
  • Treating "the key cannot be recovered" as a security property. It is satisfied by schemes that never use the key.
  • Reading correctness as a weak form of security. It is not a form of security at all.
  • Assuming an unreadable message is an unmodifiable one. The most secret scheme there is offers no protection at all against modification.
  • Saying a scheme "is secure" with no adversary attached. Secure against whom, able to do what?
  • Thinking the pad's weakness is that it is slow or old-fashioned. Its secrecy is perfect and provably so; its problems are key length, key reuse, and having no integrity whatsoever.
  • Forgetting that Tag and Enc take different keys.
