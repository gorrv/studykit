Topic 03 · Recursion

Divide, Conquer, and Solve the Recurrence

from a recursive algorithm to its running time, and back

A recursive algorithm describes its own cost recursively, and that description is a recurrence relation: T(n) in terms of T of something smaller. This topic is about turning such a relation into a closed form — by guessing and proving with induction, or by reading the answer off the Master Theorem when the recurrence has the right shape. It closes with the proof Topic 2 deferred.

The shape of the argument
Divide-and-conquer splits a problem into self-similar pieces, and the cost of doing so is a recurrence. Two ways to solve one: induction, where you guess the closed form and prove it, and the Master Theorem, which reads the answer off a, b and f(n) directly. Finally Cook–Levin shows SAT is NP-complete by writing an entire Turing machine computation as a Boolean formula.

Divide and conquer

The three steps
Divide) Split the problem into several ‘self-similar’ but smaller sub-problems.
Conquer) Solve those sub-problems recursively.
Combine) Recombine their answers into a solution for the whole.

The cost of the divide and combine steps is the f(n) that shows up in every recurrence below. The conquer step is where the recursion — and the a and b — come from.

The Towers of Hanoi

Move a tower of n discs from peg 1 to peg 3, one disc at a time, never placing a disc on a smaller one. The recursive solution is three lines, and it is pure divide-and-conquer:

MOVETOWER(n, 1, 3)
1. If n = 1 then MOVE(1, 3)
2. Else
   3. MOVETOWER(n − 1, 1, 2) get the top n−1 out of the way
   4. MOVE(1, 3) move the one disc that matters
   5. MOVETOWER(n − 1, 2, 3) put them back on top

Count the moves and the recurrence writes itself: two recursive calls on n−1, plus the single move in the middle.

The recurrence, and its solution
T(1) = 1,   T(n) = 2 T(n−1) + 1  →  T(n) = 2n − 1

Giving 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, 127, 255, … Each extra disc doubles the work and adds one move.

Merge sort

Split the list in half, sort each half recursively, then merge the two sorted halves. The merge walks both halves once, so it costs about n.

The recurrence
T(1) = 1,   T(n) = T(⌊n/2⌋) + T(⌈n/2⌉) + n

Often written in the simpler approximate form T(n) ≈ 2T(⌈n/2⌉) + n, which is what the Master Theorem below takes as its input, and which gives Θ(n log n).
The two recurrences are not the same sequence
The exact form gives 1, 4, 8, 12, 17, 22, 27, 32; the approximation gives 1, 4, 11, 12, 27, 28, 31, 32. They agree only when n is a power of two and part company from n = 3. Both are Θ(n log n), which is why the approximation is harmless for the growth rate — but if you are asked to tabulate T(n), say which one you are tabulating.

Solving by induction

The lectures call this the method of substitution: guess the closed form, then prove it. The proof has two obligations and both must be discharged.

Proof by induction
Base case) Show the formula holds at the smallest n.
Inductive case) (i) Assume it holds for n = k. (ii) Substitute, and confirm it then holds for n = k+1.

Strong induction changes only step (i): assume it holds for all m ≤ k, not just for k. Use it when the recurrence refers to something other than n−1 — anything with T(n/2) in it needs the stronger hypothesis, because k/2 is not k−1.

Here is the Hanoi recurrence solved. Notice that the inductive step never re-derives anything: it substitutes the assumption and simplifies.

T(1) = 1, T(n) = 2T(n−1) + 1  ⟹  T(n) = 2ⁿ − 1
Base case. T(1) = 1, and 2¹ − 1 = 1. ✓
I.H. Assume T(k) = 2k − 1 for some k ≥ 1.
T(k+1) = 2 T(k) + 1 the recurrence at n = k+1
        = 2(2k − 1) + 1 substitute the I.H. — this is the only place it is used
        = 2k+1 − 2 + 1
        = 2k+1 − 1 which is the formula at n = k+1  ∎
Induction proves inequalities too
Nothing requires the claim to be an equation. To show T(n) ≥ n log₂ n for T(n) = 2T(⌈n/2⌉) + n, the base case becomes T(1) = 1 ≥ 1·log₂1 = 0, and the step chains inequalities instead of equalities. The structure is identical; only the relation changes.

Practice tool

Unfold it, then test the step

Write 2T(n-1) + 1, T(floor(n/2)) + T(ceil(n/2)) + n, 2^n - 1, n*log2(n). The closed form is not just compared value by value — it is substituted into the recurrence, which is the thing an inductive step actually asks you to do.
Two ways a proof fails, and they are different
The base case fails. The step can be perfect and the result still false — a bad base propagates all the way up. Try the triangular preset with T(1) = 2: the step still holds at every k, and the formula is still wrong everywhere.

The step fails. The formula may match the first few values and then diverge. Matching values is not a proof, which is why the tool substitutes rather than just compares.

The Master Theorem

Guessing works, but for the divide-and-conquer shape there is a formula that skips the guessing entirely.

The theorem to memorise
Let T be monotonically increasing with T(n) = a T(n/b) + f(n) for constants a ≥ 1, b ≥ 2. Put k = logb a. Then

Case 1) f(n) ∈ O(nk−ε)  →  T(n) = Θ(nk)
Case 2) f(n) ∈ Θ(nk)  →  T(n) = Θ(nk log n)
Case 3) f(n) ∈ Ω(nk+ε)  →  T(n) = Θ(f(n))

for some ε > 0.

The whole thing is one comparison. nk is the cost of the recursion — a branches, logb n levels deep. f(n) is the cost of the dividing and combining. Whichever grows faster wins; if they tie, every one of the logb n levels costs the same, and multiplying by the number of levels is where the extra log n comes from.

The ε is load-bearing: there is a gap between the cases
Cases 1 and 3 do not say “slower” and “faster”. They say O(nk−ε) and Ω(nk+ε) for some ε > 0 — the separation has to be polynomial, not merely some separation. A log factor is not polynomial: there is no ε > 0 with log n ∈ Ω(nε), however small you make it.

So take T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n log n. Here k = 1, and f = n log n grows faster than n — but only by a log. Case 3 needs Ω(n1+ε) and n log n is not that. Case 2 needs Θ(n) and n log n is not that either. No case applies, and that is the answer the exam wants.

The recurrence still has a solution: unfolding it gives Θ(n log² n), and in general an f of the form nk logp n gives nk logp+1 n — one more log, because every level again costs the same. That extended form is worth a sentence, but write “the Master Theorem does not apply, because f is only a logarithmic factor above nk” first. The tool below reports this case as no case rather than forcing it into Case 3; try 9, 3, n^2 log n.
k = logb a, and the base is b
b is what you divide by; a is how many subproblems you get. So it is logba. For a = 2, b = 3 that is log₃2 ≈ 0.63 — not log₂3 ≈ 1.58. The two are reciprocals, so flipping them is easy to do and hard to spot: the answer often still lands in the same case. The tool prints both values every time, so the wrong one has somewhere to be recognised.
Simplify f before you classify it
√((n+1)⁵) = (n+1)2.5 ∈ Θ(n2.5) — adding a constant to n cannot change a growth rate.
log₂(5n²) = log₂5 + 2log₂n ∈ Θ(log n) — the constant and the multiple both drop out.

Both of these are lecture examples, and in both the simplification is most of the work.

Cook–Levin: SAT is NP-complete

Topic 2 stated this and deferred the proof. Here it is, and the idea is startling: encode an entire Turing machine computation as a Boolean formula, so that the formula is satisfiable exactly when the machine accepts.

To show SAT is NP-hard, every problem X ∈ NP must reduce to it. So take an arbitrary X ∈ NP. By definition there is a non-deterministic machine M with

The starting point
M has a polynomial-length computation that accepts w  ⟺  w ∈ X

Polynomial length is the crucial part: it bounds how much tape the machine can touch and how many steps it can take, so the whole computation fits in a table of size polynomial in |w| — and a finite table can be described by a finite formula.

Three families of variables describe a computation, indexed by cell i, time t, symbol a and state q — all of them ranging only up to TM(n), which is polynomial:

VariableTrue exactly when
Ci,t,acell i of the tape holds symbol a at time t
Hi,tthe tape head is at position i at time t
Sq,tthe machine is in state q at time t

Everything a legal, accepting computation must satisfy can then be written as a constraint on those variables. The lecture gives four representative ones:

Constraints, in the machine's own vocabulary
¬(Sq4,2 ∧ Sq5,2) the machine cannot be in two states at once
(H0,3 ∨ H1,3 ∨ H2,3 ∨ H3,3) the head must be somewhere
C3,1,a → ¬C3,1,b a cell holds one symbol
(Sq1,6 ∧ H4,6 ∧ C4,6,a) → (Sq2,7 ∧ H3,7 ∧ C4,7,b) the transition function itself, one step at a time

Conjoin all of them — plus the start configuration and the requirement that some time step is accepting — into one formula FM,w. Then a satisfying assignment is an accepting computation, written out cell by cell and step by step, and

The conclusion
w ∈ X  ⟺  FM,w is satisfiable, so X ≤p SAT.

X was an arbitrary member of NP, so this holds for every X ∈ NP — which is precisely the definition of SAT being NP-hard. SAT is also in NP (Topic 2). Both halves, so SAT is NP-complete. ∎
Why this is the keystone
Every later NP-hardness proof — CLIQUE, HAMILTONIAN, and the rest — starts from SAT and uses the transitivity theorem. That is only legitimate because Cook–Levin did the hard work once, against all of NP at once. It is the only proof in the course that argues about an arbitrary machine rather than a specific problem.

Exam takeaways

What gets asked, and what loses marks
Do both halves of the induction. Base case and step. A perfect step with an unchecked base proves nothing.

Say where the I.H. is used. Markers look for the line where you substitute the assumption. If it never appears, you have not done an induction.

Use strong induction when the recurrence jumps. Anything with T(n/2) needs “for all m ≤ k”, because k/2 is not k−1.

k = logb a. Divide by b, get a pieces. Not loga b.

Simplify f(n) before classifying it. Constants, constant shifts and log multiples all drop out — and that simplification is usually most of the marks.

Case 2 is where the log comes from, and only there. Cases 1 and 3 have no log unless f itself has one.

Cook–Levin argues about an arbitrary NDTM, not a particular problem. That is what makes it apply to all of NP.
The one-paragraph version
A recursive algorithm's cost is a recurrence. Solve it by guessing a closed form and proving it by induction — base case, then substitute the hypothesis into the recurrence at k+1 — or, when it has the form a T(n/b) + f(n), by the Master Theorem: set k = logba and compare f against nk, taking the larger, with an extra log n when they tie. Hanoi gives 2T(n−1)+1 = 2ⁿ−1; merge sort gives 2T(n/2)+n = Θ(n log n). And Cook–Levin encodes an arbitrary polynomial-time NDTM computation as a Boolean formula, making SAT NP-complete and every reduction in Topic 2 legitimate.
