Facts to quote

Six one-liners
  • NFA ≡ DFA, by the subset construction. n states become at most 2n.
  • Regular ⟺ recognised by a finite automaton. Thompson's construction one way, state elimination the other.
  • aⁿbⁿ is not regular. Pigeonhole on states visited while reading the a's.
  • Pumping lemma proves non-regularity only, never regularity.
  • NDTM ≡ TM, by breadth-first simulation of the computation tree. Exponentially slower.
  • Church–Turing is a thesis, not a theorem — "finite mechanical process" is informal.

The pumping lemma, as a procedure

1 "Suppose L is regular. Let p be the pumping length." — you do not choose p.
2 Choose w ∈ L written in terms of p, e.g. w = apbp, and check |w| ≥ p.
3 "Since |xy| ≤ p, y lies entirely within the first p symbols" — that is the sentence that does the work.
4 Pump with n = 0 or n = 2 and show the result leaves L. Cover every legal y, not one example.
5 "Contradiction, so L is not regular." ∎

Topic 01 traps

Cost you marks last year
  • ε ≠ ∅. {ε} has one word; ∅ has none.
  • Rejecting an NFA word after one dead branch.
  • Giving δ : Q × Σ → Q for an NFA.
  • ab* vs (ab)*.
  • "Not pumpable ⟹ not regular" is right; "pumpable ⟹ regular" is wrong.
  • Calling a non-halting machine "rejecting". It is neither — that is the point.

Six one-liners
  • P ⊆ NP, because a deterministic machine is a non-deterministic one that never branches.
  • NP = "has a short certificate you can check fast". Name the certificate and its check cost — that is the whole membership proof.
  • DNF from the true rows, as written. CNF from the false rows, every literal flipped.
  • SAT is NP-complete (Cook–Levin), and stays so restricted to CNF input.
  • SAT ≤p CLIQUE: vertex per literal occurrence; edge iff different clauses and not complementary; k = number of clauses.
  • CLIQUE and HAMILTONIAN are NP-complete, both by reduction from SAT plus membership in NP.
Traps
  • NP means non-deterministic polynomial, not "not polynomial".
  • Giving one half of NP-completeness. Both halves, every time.
  • Reduction direction: SAT ≤p X to show X is hard. Never X ≤p SAT.
  • Forgetting the ⟺: a reduction must send no-instances to no-instances too.
  • O where Θ was asked. n ∈ O(n!) is true and useless.
  • Not flipping the literals when reading CNF off a truth table.
  • Hamiltonian (every vertex) vs Eulerian (every edge). Euler is easy; Hamilton is NP-complete.

Topic 03 · recursion

The Master Theorem, verbatim
T(n) = a T(n/b) + f(n),   a ≥ 1, b ≥ 2,   k = logb a:

Case 1f ∈ O(nk−ε)T = Θ(nk)
Case 2f ∈ Θ(nk)T = Θ(nk log n)
Case 3f ∈ Ω(nk+ε)T = Θ(f(n))

nk is the cost of the recursion, f(n) the cost of dividing and combining. The larger wins; when they tie, every one of the logb n levels costs the same and that is where the log comes from.
Recurrences worth knowing on sight
HanoiT(n) = 2T(n−1) + 12n − 1
Merge sortT(n) = 2T(n/2) + nΘ(n log n)
Binary searchT(n) = T(n/2) + 1Θ(log n)
TriangularT(n) = T(n−1) + n(n² + n)/2
FibonacciT(n) = T(n−1) + T(n−2)Θ(Φn)

Induction, as a procedure

1 Base case. Show the formula holds at the smallest n. Do not skip it — a bad base with a perfect step still gives a false result.
2 State the I.H. "Assume T(k) = … for some k ≥ 1." For strong induction: "for all m ≤ k".
3 Write the recurrence at k+1. Not the formula — the recurrence.
4 Substitute the I.H. This is the line markers look for. If it never appears, you have not done an induction.
5 Simplify to the formula at k+1. Then ∎.
Traps
  • k = logba. Divide by b, get a pieces. Not logab — the two are reciprocals and the case often still lands right, so the error hides.
  • Ordinary induction on a recurrence that jumps. Anything with T(n/2) needs strong induction, because k/2 is not k−1.
  • Not simplifying f before classifying it: √((n+1)⁵) is Θ(n2.5), log(5n²) is Θ(log n).
  • A log in the answer for Case 1 or 3. Only Case 2 introduces one.
  • Checking values instead of substituting. Matching the first few n is not a proof.
