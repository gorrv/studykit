Topic 01 · machines and languages

Write these out exactly

Configurations

MachineA configuration isAccepts when

Facts to quote

Six one-liners
  • NFA ≡ DFA, by the subset construction. n states become at most 2n.
  • Regular ⟺ recognised by a finite automaton. Thompson's construction one way, state elimination the other.
  • aⁿbⁿ is not regular. Pigeonhole on states visited while reading the a's.
  • Pumping lemma proves non-regularity only, never regularity.
  • NDTM ≡ TM, by breadth-first simulation of the computation tree. Exponentially slower.
  • Church–Turing is a thesis, not a theorem — "finite mechanical process" is informal.

The pumping lemma, as a procedure

1 "Suppose L is regular. Let p be the pumping length." — you do not choose p.
2 Choose w ∈ L written in terms of p, e.g. w = apbp, and check |w| ≥ p.
3 "Since |xy| ≤ p, y lies entirely within the first p symbols" — that is the sentence that does the work.
4 Pump with n = 0 or n = 2 and show the result leaves L. Cover every legal y, not one example.
5 "Contradiction, so L is not regular." ∎

Topic 01 traps

Cost you marks last year
  • ε ≠ ∅. {ε} has one word; ∅ has none.
  • Rejecting an NFA word after one dead branch.
  • Giving δ : Q × Σ → Q for an NFA.
  • ab* vs (ab)*.
  • "Not pumpable ⟹ not regular" is right; "pumpable ⟹ regular" is wrong.
  • Calling a non-halting machine "rejecting". It is neither — that is the point.

Six one-liners
  • P ⊆ NP, because a deterministic machine is a non-deterministic one that never branches.
  • NP = "has a short certificate you can check fast". Name the certificate and its check cost — that is the whole membership proof.
  • DNF from the true rows, as written. CNF from the false rows, every literal flipped.
  • SAT is NP-complete (Cook–Levin), and stays so restricted to CNF input.
  • SAT ≤p CLIQUE: vertex per literal occurrence; edge iff different clauses and not complementary; k = number of clauses.
  • CLIQUE and HAMILTONIAN are NP-complete, both by reduction from SAT plus membership in NP.
Traps
  • NP means non-deterministic polynomial, not "not polynomial".
  • Giving one half of NP-completeness. Both halves, every time.
  • Reduction direction: SAT ≤p X to show X is hard. Never X ≤p SAT.
  • Forgetting the ⟺: a reduction must send no-instances to no-instances too.
  • O where Θ was asked. n ∈ O(n!) is true and useless.
  • Not flipping the literals when reading CNF off a truth table.
  • Hamiltonian (every vertex) vs Eulerian (every edge). Euler is easy; Hamilton is NP-complete.

Topic 03 · recursion

The Master Theorem, verbatim
T(n) = a T(n/b) + f(n),   a ≥ 1, b ≥ 2,   k = logb a:

Case 1f ∈ O(nk−ε)T = Θ(nk)
Case 2f ∈ Θ(nk)T = Θ(nk log n)
Case 3f ∈ Ω(nk+ε)T = Θ(f(n))

nk is the cost of the recursion, f(n) the cost of dividing and combining. The larger wins; when they tie, every one of the logb n levels costs the same and that is where the log comes from.

The ε matters. Cases 1 and 3 need a polynomial separation, and a log factor is not one — there is no ε > 0 with log n ∈ Ω(nε). So for T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n log n no case applies at all: f is above nk but not polynomially above, and not Θ(nk) either. Say that. (Unfolding gives Θ(n log² n); in general nk logp n gives nk logp+1 n.)
Recurrences worth knowing on sight
HanoiT(n) = 2T(n−1) + 12n − 1
Merge sortT(n) = 2T(n/2) + nΘ(n log n)
Binary searchT(n) = T(n/2) + 1Θ(log n)
TriangularT(n) = T(n−1) + n(n² + n)/2
FibonacciT(n) = T(n−1) + T(n−2)Θ(Φn)

Induction, as a procedure

1 Base case. Show the formula holds at the smallest n. Do not skip it — a bad base with a perfect step still gives a false result.
2 State the I.H. "Assume T(k) = … for some k ≥ 1." For strong induction: "for all m ≤ k".
3 Write the recurrence at k+1. Not the formula — the recurrence.
4 Substitute the I.H. This is the line markers look for. If it never appears, you have not done an induction.
5 Simplify to the formula at k+1. Then ∎.
Traps
  • k = logba. Divide by b, get a pieces. Not logab — the two are reciprocals and the case often still lands right, so the error hides.
  • Ordinary induction on a recurrence that jumps. Anything with T(n/2) needs strong induction, because k/2 is not k−1.
  • Not simplifying f before classifying it: √((n+1)⁵) is Θ(n2.5), log(5n²) is Θ(log n).
  • A log in the answer for Case 1 or 3. Only Case 2 introduces one.
  • Checking values instead of substituting. Matching the first few n is not a proof.

Topic 04 · graph algorithms

The four termination times
BFS / DFSO(|V| + |E|)∑u|Adj(u)| = O(|E|)
KruskalO(|E| log |E|)dominated by the sort
PrimO(|E| + |V| log |V|)with a Fibonacci heap
Topological sort / SCCO(|V| + |E|)DFS once, or twice
Six one-liners
  • BFS and DFS are one algorithm with a queue or a stack. BFS gives shortest paths; DFS gives timestamps and back edges.
  • The linear bound comes from summing |Adj(u)|, not multiplying it by |V|.
  • “Would this edge make a cycle?” = “are u and v already connected?” — a disjoint-set lookup, not a search.
  • A spanning tree of |V| vertices always has |V| − 1 edges. What is minimised is the weight.
  • A topological order exists ⟺ the graph is a DAG. Same question, two phrasings.
  • GSCC is always a DAG — a cycle among components would have merged them.
SCC, in four steps
1) DFS on G, record the reverse finishing order.
2) Take the first unassigned vertex u in that order.
3) DFS on the transpose GT from u; everything reached is one component.
4) Remove them and repeat.

Pass 1 finds what u reaches; pass 2 finds what reaches u. Both = a round trip.
Traps
  • O(|V|·|E|) for a search, from multiplying step 4 by the loop.
  • Assuming the MST is unique — Kruskal and Prim can return different trees of equal weight.
  • Assuming the topological order is unique — the algorithm says any unsorted vertex.
  • Forgetting maximal in the SCC definition.
  • Second SCC pass on G rather than GT.
  • Naming no cycle when refusing to topologically sort.

Topic 05 · SAT solving

The three responses to an NP-complete problem
GREEDY-SATpolynomialincomplete — False means “stuck”
DPLLexponential worst casecomplete — always right
2SAT, Hornpolynomialcomplete, but only for restricted input
The two DPLL rules, kept apart
Unit propagation — a clause with one literal left. Assigns what MUST be assigned. Can conflict, and that is how dead branches are found.

Pure literal elimination — a literal with only one sign in the whole formula. Assigns what SHOULD be assigned. Can never conflict.

Neither is needed for correctness. Plain branching is already complete. They exist to make the search stop sooner.
The reductions, and where the line falls
2SAT ≤p 3SAT ≤p … ≤p SAT  — trivial, identity map.
SAT ≤p 3SAT  — split wide clauses with fresh bridging variables: (l₁ ∨ l₂ ∨ X) ∧ (¬X ∨ l₃ ∨ l₄). A k-literal clause becomes k−2 clauses using k−3 fresh variables.
3SAT ≤p 2SAT  — no. 2SAT is in P, so this would prove P = NP.
2SAT by implication graph
1) (a ∨ b) becomes ¬a → b and ¬b → a.
2) One vertex per literal, one edge per implication.
3) Compute the SCCs.
4) Unsatisfiable ⟺ some component contains a literal and its own negation.

To read off an assignment: make a literal true when its component is downstream of its negation’s.
Horn clauses
At most one positive literal, so it reads as a rule: (¬P ∨ ¬Q ∨ R) is P ∧ Q → R.
Decided by forward chaining — fire from the facts to a fixed point, linear time — because a Horn formula has a unique minimal model.

2SAT and Horn overlap rather than nest: (P ∨ Q) is 2SAT not Horn; (¬P ∨ ¬Q ∨ R) is Horn not 2SAT.
Traps
  • Reading “polynomial time” as “solves it”. Greedy is the counterexample, and it is the standard one.
  • Swapping the two rules. Unit = one literal left in a clause; pure = one sign across the formula.
  • Claiming pure literal elimination can conflict. It cannot.
  • Reusing an existing variable as the 3SAT bridge. It must be fresh.
  • Giving one direction of a ≤p argument.
  • “2SAT is easy because clauses are small” — 3SAT’s are small too. It is easy because two literals make an implication.

Topic 07 · approximation & TSP

Write these out exactly
TSPdec — complete weighted graph (V,d) and a value D; True iff some Hamiltonian cycle has length ≤ D. NP-complete.
TSP — the same graph; return the shortest Hamiltonian cycle.

RM(w) = max( Capprox/Cglobal , Cglobal/Capprox ) ≥ 1
R-approximable: a polynomial-time M with RM(w) ≤ R for all w.
Unapproximable: not R-approximable for any fixed R.
The R = 2 proof, as a chain
length_of(Happrox) ≤ length_of(H0)  (swaps only shorten)
  ≤ 2 · length_of(T0)  (preorder walks each tree edge twice)
  ≤ 2 · length_of(T)  (T0 is a MINIMUM spanning tree)
  ≤ 2 · length_of(Hglobal)  (T is the optimal tour minus an edge)

The factor of 2 is earned at line two, by the traversal — not by the swaps. And line two needs the triangle inequality, so the theorem is about metric TSP. Christofides (1976) improves the ratio to 3/2.
Unapproximability, in one line
Given a HAMILTONIAN instance and a claimed ratio R, set d = 1 on edges and nR + 1 on non-edges. Then the cheapest tour is ≤ nR iff G has a Hamiltonian cycle — so an R-approximation would decide an NP-complete problem in polynomial time. The penalty is chosen after R, which is why no R works.
Traps
  • “TSP is 2-approximable” without metric. Unqualified it is false.
  • Treating a ratio as typical rather than worst-case. Examples refute; only proofs establish.
  • Thinking the swaps earn the factor of 2. They earn nothing; they only avoid harm.
  • Reconnecting a 2-opt swap so each path closes on itself — two cycles, not a tour.
  • Forgetting TSP needs a complete graph.
  • Confusing 2OPT’s local optimum with the global one.

Topic 08 · linear & integer programming

Write these out exactly
LP — Input: constraints Ax ≤ b and a linear f : ℝn → ℝ. Output: x ∈ (ℝ≥0)n satisfying them with f(x) maximal.
IP — identical, but x ∈ (ℤ≥0)n.

Feasible region R = { x ∈ ℝn : x is a solution }.
Optimal set { x ∈ R : ∀y ∈ R, f(x) ≥ f(y) } ⊆ R.

Non-negativity is implicit in both. Inequalities are non-strict, or the optimum need not exist.
Simplex, in seven lines
1 Slack form: a·x ≤ b becomes a·x + s = b, so s = b − a·x, with s ≥ 0 iff the constraint holds and s = 0 iff it is tight. Cost row holds the objective negated.
2 Pivot column = most negative entry in the final row.
3 Quotients = right-hand side ÷ pivot-column entry.
4 Pivot row = smallest quotient with a positive denominator and a non-negative numerator.
5 Rpiv ← (1/pivot)·Rpiv; every other Ri ← Ri − (Ri-entry / pivot)·Rpiv.
6 Repeat until no negatives remain in the final row.
7 Read off: a column that is all zeros bar a single 1 is basic and takes that row's right-hand side; everything else is 0.
Facts to quote
The optimum of a linear objective over a convex region is attained at a vertex — which is why checking corners works at all, and why there are three possible outcomes: a unique solution, infinitely many (contour parallel to an edge), or none (region empty, or unbounded in an improving direction).

Klee–Minty (1972) — simplex is exponential in the worst case; their program takes 2n+1 − 1 pivots on n+1 variables.
Khachiyan (1979) — LP is solvable in polynomial time, by the ellipsoid method. Not a statement about simplex.
IP is NP-hard — by SAT ≤p IP.
SAT ≤p IP, in one line
Two variables xP, x¬P per propositional variable. Each clause becomes sum of its literals' variables ≥ 1. Each variable gets both xP + x¬P ≤ 1 and ≥ 1, which force the sum to 1 and so — over the integers — force exactly one of them to be 1. Drop integrality and xP = x¬P = ½ satisfies everything, which is the whole difference between LP and IP in one fraction.
BRANCH-AND-BOUND
Solve the relaxation. Integral ⟹ done. Otherwise pick a fractional xi = ai and recurse on LP ∪ {xi ≤ ⌊ai⌋} and LP ∪ {xi ≥ ⌈ai⌉}, returning whichever child maximises f. The branches are disjoint and lose no integer point.

The relaxation's optimum is an upper bound on the integer optimum — that is what the bound in the name is for: prune any node that cannot beat the best integer solution so far.
Traps
  • Forgetting x ≥ 0. It supplies two edges of the region and usually two of the vertices.
  • Not negating the objective in the cost row.
  • Sign errors converting 2y − x ≤ 5 — the row is (−1, 2), not (1, 2).
  • Requiring the quotient's numerator to be strictly positive. Zero is allowed, and skipping it can leave the tableau infeasible.
  • Rounding a relaxation's answer to get the integer one. It is an upper bound; the integer optimum is often at a different point entirely.
  • Saying integer programming is NP-complete when the reduction gives NP-hard.
  • Claiming Khachiyan made simplex polynomial. Different algorithm.
  • Starting the tableau on a program with a ≥ constraint. There is no feasible corner at the origin; that needs a phase-one method.

Topic 09 · probability & randomised classes

Write these out exactly
E[X] = Σk k · pX(k)
Var[X] = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − E[X]²,   σX = √Var[X]
E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b  and  E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

Markov. For a non-negative X and any a > 0: Prob( X ≥ a·E[X] ) ≤ 1/a.

PTM. ⟨Σ, Q, qinit, qacc, qrej, δ⟩ with δ(state, symbol) ↦ a distribution over Q × (Σ ∪ {␣}) × {←, →}. Prob(branch) = product along it; Prob(accept) = sum over accepting branches.
BPP and ZPP
w ∉ Lw ∈ Ltime
BPPProb(accept) ≤ 1/3Prob(accept) ≥ 2/3 T(n) ∈ O(nk) worst case
ZPPProb(accept) = 0Prob(accept) = 1 E[T(n)] ∈ O(nk) expected

BPP = Monte Carlo (on time, maybe wrong). ZPP = Las Vegas (always right, maybe slow).

ZPP ⊆ BPP: run the Las Vegas machine for K = 3·E[T]; if unfinished, flip a fair coin. Markov caps Prob(T > K) at 1/3, which lands inside BPP’s bounds.

Known: P ⊆ ZPP ⊆ BPP ⊆ PSpace. Open: BPP ⊆ NP? NP ⊆ BPP? ZPP = P? BPP = P? — the last is widely believed true.
Traps
  • Quoting Markov without X ≥ 0. It is commonly dropped, and it is false without it.
  • Thinking E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] needs independence. It needs nothing.
  • Thinking Var[X + Y] = Var[X] + Var[Y] needs nothing. It needs independence.
  • Defining variance as E[X − μ] — always zero, for every X.
  • Swapping which class has the time guarantee. BPP’s clock is exact; ZPP’s answer is.
  • Treating 1/3 and 2/3 as essential. Any constant gap around ½ amplifies by majority vote.
  • Confusing "halts with probability 1" with "halts within a bound". They differ, and that gap is the gap between ZPP and BPP.

Topic 10 · the limits of computation

Write these out exactly
M(w) = 1 if M accepts, 0 if M rejects, ↑ if M does not halt.

Decidable — some M is sound (M(w) = 1 ⇒ w ∈ L), complete (w ∈ L ⇒ M(w) = 1) and terminating (TM(w) < ∞ for all w).
Recognisable — sound and complete, termination not required.

Church–Turing thesis. Any language effectively computable by a finite process is recognisable by a Turing machine. A thesis, not a theorem.

Encodings. ⟨w₀, …, wₙ⟩ = #w₀#…#wₙ#; a finite function as a tuple of pairs; code(M) = ⟨qinit, qacc, qrej, ⟨δ⟩⟩.
Universal machine. Mu(⟨code(M), w⟩) = M(w).
The two proofs
An undecidable language exists. Enumerate words w₀, w₁, …, and machines M₀, M₁, …. Put L = { wi : Mi does not accept wi }. Any Mj differs from L at wj, so no machine has L as its language.

HALTTM is undecidable. Assume halt. Build M* which clones its input, asks halt, then loops if halt says "halts" and accepts if it says "does not". Then M*(code(M*)) halts ⟺ it does not halt. ∎

Others: ATM, RTM, ETM, EQTM, REGULARTM, and the Entscheidungsproblem (is a predicate formula a tautology?) — the last via Cook–Levin plus ∃t, reducing ATM to the complement of validity.
Traps
  • Saying halting is "too hard". It is impossible, not expensive.
  • Getting the reduction backwards. Reduce the known-undecidable problem TO the new one.
  • Confusing undecidable with NP-hard. NP-hard problems are all decidable.
  • Thinking the counting argument names an undecidable language. It only shows one exists.
  • Forgetting HALT is recognisable — you can confirm "yes" and never "no".
  • Calling Church–Turing a theorem.
